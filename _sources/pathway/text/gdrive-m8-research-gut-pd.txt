Your Gut & Parkinson’s Disease
What the Research Says — In Plain Language
You may already know that Parkinson’s affects movement — but did you know it also has
a deep connection to your gut? Researchers are discovering that the health of your
digestive system plays a much bigger role in Parkinson’s than anyone realized. Here is
what the science says, in plain language.
■ The Gut–Brain Connection: Why Your Gut Matters
Your gut and your brain are in constant communication through a pathway researchers call the
gut–brain axis. Think of it as a two-way highway between your digestive system and your
brain, sending signals back and forth all day long.
In Parkinson’s, this communication system can be disrupted. Research has found that people
with Parkinson’s often have changes in their gut microbiome — the trillions of bacteria that
live in your digestive tract. These changes can affect inflammation, brain health, and many of
the non-motor symptoms you may experience.
“The gut microbiota is strongly associated with Parkinson’s disease. Intestinal issues
often occur before motor symptoms appear — suggesting the gut may play a role early in
the disease process.” — Translational Neurodegeneration, 2023
One protein central to Parkinson’s — called alpha-synuclein — is believed to potentially
begin accumulating in the gut and travel to the brain via the vagus nerve (the main nerve
connecting gut and brain). This is an active and exciting area of research.

■ Constipation: A Common Challenge in Parkinson’s
Constipation is one of the most common non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s, and it is often
under-discussed. Research shows that gut movement (motility) is frequently slowed in people
with Parkinson’s, partly because dopamine — the same brain chemical affected by PD — also
plays a role in regulating the digestive system.
Good news: Studies show that dietary changes can meaningfully improve constipation in people
with Parkinson’s — sometimes within just a few weeks.
■ Study #1: Mediterranean Diet — 5 Weeks to Fewer Symptoms
What researchers did:
Scientists at the University of Florida followed people with Parkinson’s who followed a
Mediterranean diet for just 5 weeks. They measured constipation scores, gut bacteria
composition, and other digestive markers before and after.
What they found:
• Constipation scores significantly improved after 5 weeks
• Gut bacteria changed in helpful ways — beneficial bacteria increased, while potentially
harmful strains decreased
• Participants also experienced weight loss
• The diet was shown to be realistic and achievable for people with PD
Key takeaway: Just 5 weeks of eating a Mediterranean diet made a measurable difference
in gut health and constipation for people living with Parkinson’s.

■ Study #2: A Larger, Stronger Study Confirms the Results (2024)
Because the original 2021 study was small, the same research team returned with a larger,
more rigorous randomized controlled trial — the gold standard of medical research.
Published in September 2024, it compared people following a Mediterranean diet against a
standard care group over 8 weeks.
What they found:
• Both groups saw reduced constipation — but the Mediterranean diet group consumed
significantly more dietary fibre
• The Mediterranean diet group showed lower intestinal inflammation (measured by a
marker called calprotectin)
• This suggests the Mediterranean diet does more than just relieve symptoms — it may
actually reduce inflammation in the gut
Why does gut inflammation matter? Chronic inflammation in the gut is thought to contribute
to Parkinson’s disease progression. Reducing it through diet is a simple, safe, and powerful tool.
Source: Rusch et al., Nutrients, September 2024 — University of Florida, Norman Fixel Institute for Neurological Diseases
■ Study #3: A Review of All the Evidence (2024)
A 2024 review from University College London looked at all available studies on
Mediterranean diet, gut microbiome, and Parkinson’s together. Reviewing both observational
studies (over 1,200 participants) and clinical trials, they found consistent patterns across the
research.

■ What Does the Mediterranean Diet Actually Look Like?
The good news: this is not a restrictive or complicated diet. It focuses on whole, natural foods
that are colourful, flavourful, and satisfying.
Eat Plenty Of Limit or Reduce
• Vegetables — as many colours as possible
• Fresh fruit
• Whole grains (oats, brown rice, quinoa)
• Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, beans)
• Nuts and seeds
• Olive oil as your main fat
• Fish, especially fatty fish (salmon, sardines)
• Herbs and spices for flavour
• Ultra-processed foods
• Fast food and fried foods
• Sugary drinks and sweets
• Red meat (occasional is fine)
• Highly refined white bread/pasta
• Packaged snack foods

Beyond diet, researchers are exploring other ways to support gut health in Parkinson’s. While
these are still emerging, the early findings are encouraging:
• Probiotics and prebiotics — beneficial bacteria supplements may help improve bowel
motility and reduce gut inflammation
• Resistant starch — a type of fibre found in foods like cooled potatoes, unripe bananas, and
oats has been studied in a clinical trial (RESISTA-PD) for its effects on gut bacteria in
Parkinson’s
• Fecal microbiome transplant (FMT) — transferring healthy gut bacteria from a donor into
a patient’s gut; early studies show promise for constipation relief, though this is still
experimental
Bottom line: Diet remains the most practical, safe, and evidence-supported tool available right
now for supporting gut health in Parkinson’s.
■ What You Can Start Doing Today
• Add one more vegetable to your plate at lunch and dinner
• Swap refined bread or pasta for a whole grain version
• Add a handful of nuts or seeds as a daily snack
• Use olive oil instead of butter when cooking
• Aim for fish twice a week — salmon, sardines, or trout are great choices
• Drink water consistently throughout the day to support bowel regularity
• Add legumes (lentil soup, chickpea salad) to your weekly meals
You do not need to overhaul your entire diet overnight. Small, consistent changes add up.
Research shows that even short-term improvement in eating patterns can produce
meaningful changes in your gut health.

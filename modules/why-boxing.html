Why Boxing?Pourquoi la boxe?

14 min read Updated 2026

Boxing isn't just an exercise trend — for people living with Parkinson's and for aging adults, it's one of the most evidence-backed physical interventions available. This lesson walks through what the research says, how boxing works on the brain and body, why our technique is deliberately different from traditional boxing, and how to explain all of it with confidence to your clients.

What you'll learn

  • Summarise the current research evidence for boxing in Parkinson's — including the 2025 systematic review and the 2026 ACSM and Parkinson's Foundation endorsement.
  • Explain neuroplasticity and why high-intensity exercise may do more than manage symptoms.
  • Connect each physical and mental benefit of boxing to a specific symptom of Parkinson's or aging.
  • Describe how the Boxing 4 Health technique intentionally differs from traditional boxing — and why that difference is therapeutic.

Section 01

The Research Foundation

A 2025 systematic review published in Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience analyzed 13 studies on boxing exercise for Parkinson's disease and found consistent evidence that boxing improves gait, mobility, and balance in people with PD. The majority of these studies were published between 2020 and 2023 — this is a rapidly growing and strengthening body of evidence.

Most importantly, high-intensity exercise like boxing may actually slow Parkinson's progression by promoting neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to form new connections and adapt. This places boxing in a unique category: not just symptom management, but potentially disease-modifying.

Section 02

Officially Recommended: ACSM & the Parkinson's Foundation

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) — the world's largest sports medicine and exercise science organization — and the Parkinson's Foundation published updated exercise recommendations in 2026 following extensive review by subject matter experts and PD community members.

Under Balance, Agility & Multitasking (BAM) — recommended 2–3 days per week — the guidelines specifically list boxing as a recommended activity type, alongside yoga, tai chi, and dance. The guidelines note that BAM training should include multidirectional stepping, weight shifting, turning, large movements, and multitasking. That describes a B4H class exactly.

Aerobic Activity

3 days/week, 30+ minutes at moderate-to-vigorous intensity. Continuous, rhythmic activities.

Strength Training

2–3 non-consecutive days/week, 30+ minutes. Major muscle groups, 2–3 sets of 8–10 reps.

Stretching

2–3 days/week, with daily stretching most effective. Active range of motion with deep breathing.

Balance, Agility & Multitasking — That's Us

2–3 days/week. Multidirectional stepping, large movements, multitasking. Specifically includes boxing.

Section 03

Boxing & the Brain: Neuroplasticity

Parkinson's disease is caused by the progressive loss of dopamine-producing neurons. While medication manages symptoms, exercise is the only intervention shown to potentially promote neuroplasticity — meaning it may help the brain compensate and adapt at a structural level.

Boxing is classified as high-intensity exercise. Research strongly suggests that high-intensity exercise promotes neuroplasticity in people with PD in ways that low-intensity exercise does not. This is why the intensity of our classes is not just about fitness — it's neurologically purposeful.

A 2023 pilot study from Rush University Medical Center (Patel et al.) found that people with PD who completed a 12-week community-based boxing program showed improvements in both motor and non-motor symptoms — including mood, sleep, and cognitive function.

Good to know

Sources: Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience (2025) · Patel, Blasucci & Mahajan, Journal of Clinical Neuroscience (2023) · Blacker et al., PM&R (2024).

Section 04

What Boxing Develops

Every one of these benefits directly addresses a symptom of Parkinson's or aging. Each card pairs the benefit with the research behind it.

Balance

Weight shifting, footwork, and reactive movements in boxing directly train the postural control systems most affected by PD. Balance is one of the most consistently improved outcomes in boxing research.

Multiple studies confirm balance improvements in PD following boxing programs, including Moore et al. (2021), Domingos et al. (2022), and Sonne et al. (2021). The Fullerton Advanced Balance Scale — the same tool we use in our assessments — was used to measure these improvements.

Amplitude

Amplitude training — doing movements BIG — is one of the most important principles in PD exercise. Boxing naturally encourages large, powerful, full-range movements. Think big punches, big steps, big voice.

Amplitude-based exercise (also called LSVT BIG) is a clinically established approach in PD rehabilitation. Research shows that people with PD have reduced amplitude of movement and that training "big" helps rewire the brain's sense of normal movement size. Boxing is an ideal vehicle for this because every technique — jab, cross, hook — benefits from amplitude.

Hand-Eye Coordination

Hitting a moving pad or target requires the brain to integrate visual information with motor output in real time — a demanding dual-task that exercises the neural pathways most compromised by PD.

The dual-task demands of boxing (moving, watching a target, planning a punch, executing it) closely mirror the challenges PD clients face in daily life. Research on dual-task training in PD consistently shows improvements in both motor performance and cognitive function. This is why hitting mitts is therapeutically superior to simply punching the air.

Voice & Speech

Boxing requires exertion — and exertion naturally increases vocal output. Calling out combinations, counting, and the effort of intense exercise all strengthen respiratory muscles and vocal projection.

A 2023 pilot study by Watts et al. (Journal of Clinical Medicine) specifically examined boxing's effect on voice and phonation in PD, finding improvements in respiratory pressure and voice aerodynamics. Hypophonia (soft voice) is a common and frustrating PD symptom — boxing addresses it both directly and indirectly through breathing and effort demands.

Agility & Speed

Footwork drills, rapid direction changes, and quick combination sequences train the fast-twitch neuromuscular system — which is significantly slowed by bradykinesia in Parkinson's.

Bradykinesia (slowness of movement) is one of the cardinal symptoms of PD. High-intensity, speed-based exercise creates a neurological demand for faster motor output, which research suggests can partially offset bradykinesia-related declines. The circuit format of Fighter classes is designed specifically to maintain this intensity demand.

Strength & Power

Generating force against a bag or pads builds upper and lower body strength. Resistance and power training also supports bone density, fall recovery, and functional independence.

Muscle weakness is a common secondary symptom of PD, compounded by inactivity. The FIGHT-PD feasibility study (Blacker et al., PM&R, 2024) documented improvements in lower extremity strength using 30-second sit-to-stand tests — the same test in our B4H assessment battery. Strength gains directly translate to reduced fall risk and improved daily function.

Endurance

Sustained cardiovascular output during a full B4H class improves aerobic fitness — which is directly linked to better motor function, mood, and cognitive performance in people with PD.

Cardiovascular fitness is strongly associated with neuroplasticity. Aerobic exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — a protein that supports neuron survival and growth. In PD populations, improved cardiorespiratory fitness is associated with better motor exam scores and slower disease progression. The 6-minute walk test, used in multiple boxing studies, consistently shows improvement.

Accuracy & Footwork

Precise targeting drills train fine motor control and spatial awareness. Footwork patterns improve gait, stride length, and the shuffling walk that characterizes Parkinson's.

Gait improvements — including stride length, walking speed, and the dual-task TUG (Timed Up and Go) — are among the most consistently reported outcomes in boxing research for PD. Studies by Combs et al. (2013) and Sangarapillai et al. (2021) both found significant gait improvements following boxing programs compared to control interventions.

Flexibility

The rotational demands of boxing — reaching, twisting, extending — work against the rigidity and stooped posture that PD creates. Cool-down stretching compounds these gains.

Rigidity is a cardinal symptom of PD — muscles become stiff, reducing range of motion and contributing to the characteristic stooped posture. Boxing's rotational, multi-directional movements directly counteract this. Combined with our cool-down ball work and stretching, flexibility is addressed at both ends of every class.

Section 05

Mental Health & Wellbeing

Stress Relief & Mood

Hitting a heavy bag is one of the most instinctively satisfying physical releases available. Beyond the feeling, intense exercise triggers a sustained release of endorphins, dopamine, and serotonin — the exact neurotransmitters that PD depletes. A boxing class isn't just exercise; it's a neurochemical reset.

Patel et al. (2023) found non-motor symptom improvements including mood following a 12-week boxing program. Wu et al. found exercise significantly reduces depressive symptoms in PD populations.

Sleep

Poor sleep is a common and debilitating non-motor symptom of PD. Regular high-intensity exercise is one of the most evidence-supported non-pharmacological interventions for sleep quality. A 2020 pilot study by Urrutia et al. specifically examined boxing's effect on sleep in PD and found positive improvements in both sleep quality and daytime sleepiness.

Urrutia et al. (2020) — Topics in Geriatric Rehabilitation: Boxing to improve sleep quality and daytime sleepiness in PD.

Community & Belonging

A 2024 qualitative study from Denmark found that participants in Parkinson's boxing programs reported a strong sense of community, joy, gratitude, and improved mental wellbeing — not just physical improvement. The shared diagnosis, shared effort, and shared victories created bonds that participants described as profoundly meaningful.

Danish Parkinson Boxing qualitative study, Disability & Rehabilitation (published online May 2024).

Cognitive Function

The dual-task nature of boxing — simultaneously moving, thinking, reacting, and counting — places significant demands on executive function and working memory. Regular dual-task training has been shown to improve cognitive performance in older adults and people with PD, slowing the decline that often accompanies the disease.

Multiple included studies tracked cognitive outcomes via the MDS-UPDRS III and PDQ-39, showing improvements in cognitive subscores following boxing programs.

Section 06

B4H Technique: What Makes Us Different

We teach boxing — but our approach is intentionally different from traditional boxing technique. Understanding why makes you a better instructor.

Traditional Boxing

  • Hands always up — chin down, elbows in.
  • Rounded, protective shoulders — guards the face.
  • Constant tension — ready to absorb or deflect blows.
  • Compact, closed posture — minimizes target area.
  • Great for the ring — works against PD clients.

Boxing 4 Health Approach

  • Strong, upright posture — chest open, spine tall.
  • Opening movements throughout — counteract PD's forward flexion.
  • Full extension on punches — amplitude is the goal.
  • Relaxed between combinations — reduce rigidity, not add to it.
  • High intensity — without reinforcing poor posture.

Good to know

The key principle: People with Parkinson's already fight against forward flexion, rounded shoulders, and stooped posture every day. The last thing we want is to design a class that reinforces those patterns. Every opening stretch, every tall stance cue, every full-extension punch is intentionally working against the postural pull of PD — not with it.

Section 07

Styles We Incorporate

Boxing

Core of every class. Jabs, crosses, hooks, uppercuts. Focus on amplitude, extension, and posture.

Kickboxing

Lower body engagement, hip mobility, balance challenges. Added where instructor experience allows.

Muay Thai

Clinch work, elbows, knees where appropriate. Broader range of motion and full-body engagement.

Coach's Corner

Not all instructors have a boxing background — and that's okay. Teach what you know confidently and safely. What matters most is that you understand the purpose behind each movement — amplitude, posture, dual-tasking — not just the technique itself. A client doing a big, tall, open jab incorrectly is getting more therapeutic benefit than one doing a perfect traditional jab with rounded shoulders.

Key takeaways

  • The evidence base is real and growing — a 2025 systematic review of 13 studies found boxing consistently improves gait, mobility, and balance in PD, and it is now officially recommended by ACSM and the Parkinson's Foundation (2026).
  • High-intensity exercise like boxing may do more than manage symptoms — it promotes neuroplasticity and may help slow disease progression, which is why class intensity is neurologically purposeful.
  • Every boxing benefit maps to a PD or aging symptom: balance, amplitude, coordination, voice, agility, strength, endurance, footwork, and flexibility.
  • Boxing also supports mental health — mood, sleep, community, and cognition — not just physical function.
  • B4H technique deliberately opposes traditional boxing's rounded, closed posture; we cue tall, open, fully extended movements to counteract the postural pull of PD.
  • You don't need a boxing background — understanding the therapeutic purpose behind each movement matters more than perfect technique.

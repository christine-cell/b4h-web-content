Cueing StudentsGuider les élèves

7 min read Updated 2026

As Parkinson's symptoms progress, the need for effective cueing becomes more and more critical. The three essential cueing techniques — Vocal, Mirroring, and Physical Feedback — are your most powerful tools for connecting with a person living with Parkinson's and getting the best out of every session. Used well, and often together, they help clients move bigger, safer, and with more confidence than words alone ever could.

What you'll learn

  • Use your voice with clarity, repetition, and energy to cut through cognitive fog and hearing changes.
  • Mirror movements directly in front of a client so they can follow at full amplitude.
  • Apply physical feedback safely — always inside a framework of explicit, ongoing consent.
  • Combine all three techniques to create multi-sensory cueing that reaches your most affected clients.

Cueing Technique 01

Vocal

Using your voice in a clear and direct manner cuts through the noise and communicates your points effectively. For clients with Parkinson's — who may be processing information more slowly, managing cognitive fog, or dealing with hearing changes — clarity and repetition are everything.

Your clients will feed off the energy you bring to class. Take a moment to centre yourself before you begin, and then project as much excitement, positivity, and confidence as possible. Your mood is contagious — if you're flat, the room is flat. If you're fired up, they're fired up.

Be clear & direct

No long explanations during movement. Short, punchy cues that land immediately: "Punch BIG!" "Step wider!" "Chin up!" The more words you use, the less they hear.

Embrace repetition

Repeat the important points as many times as needed until the message gets across. What feels repetitive to you is often the first time it fully registers for a client managing cognitive symptoms. Never say something important just once.

Project confidence & positivity

Clients with PD often experience anxiety, self-doubt, and fear of falling. Your confident, positive tone creates psychological safety — it tells them they're in good hands and that they can do this.

Centre yourself first

If you're stressed, rushed, or distracted, your clients will feel it. Before class, take a breath, reset, and bring your best energy to the room. They deserve it — and so do you.

Coach's Corner

Before you say a word, take a moment to centre yourself. Your energy is the first cue clients receive — they read your mood before they process your instructions. Walk in with excitement and calm confidence, and the room follows.

Cueing Technique 02

Mirroring

Over and above a simple demonstration, mirroring the movement directly in front of a client is a vital tool — especially for the most affected clients. When someone with Parkinson's can see exactly what they need to do, performed at full amplitude by their trusted instructor, it brings a sense of calm and comfort that words alone can't provide.

Good to know

Mirroring means you move opposite to them. If you want them to raise their right hand, you raise your left — so from their perspective it looks correct. Position yourself directly in front of your client, make eye contact, and move together.

Position directly in front

Get face-to-face with the client you're cueing. Not beside them, not behind them — directly in front so they can see your whole body and follow the movement without having to turn.

Make it as BIG as possible

Exaggerate every movement. Full extension. Exaggerated steps. Maximum arm swing. Your amplitude sets the target — they aim for you. If you mirror small, they move small.

Use it for your most affected clients

Mirroring is particularly powerful for clients who are struggling with a movement or having an OFF period. Drop everything else and get in front of them — your body becomes their guide.

Create calm & confidence

Seeing their trusted instructor right in front of them, moving together, is deeply reassuring. It removes the guesswork and the anxiety of figuring out what to do — they just follow you.

Cueing Technique 03

Physical Feedback

The sense of touch, used alongside vocal and visual cues, can unlock movement that nothing else can reach. Something as simple as tapping on a body part can bring enough awareness to help a client find greater range, ability, and balance. For PD clients who have lost proprioceptive awareness, physical feedback can be the missing link.

Safety

Consent is non-negotiable. Always establish consent before putting your hands on a client's body — every single time. This is not optional. Physical contact is a powerful teaching tool, but it must be used within a framework of respect and informed consent.

  1. Ask explicitly the first time

    "Is it okay if I place my hand on your hip to show you the rotation?" Never assume.

  2. Consent may become implied

    As you develop comfort and trust with regular clients, consent may become implied — but even then, it still needs to be confirmed.

  3. Confirm every time

    Confirm every time you want to use physical contact as a teaching aid. A simple "Can I just show you?" before touching is always enough.

  4. Respect a no immediately

    Respect a no immediately and without question. If a client declines, move to vocal or mirroring cues without comment.

A tap creates awareness

You don't always need to guide or correct — sometimes a simple tap on the muscle group you want activated is enough to bring a client's attention there and unlock the movement. Try tapping the hip, the shoulder, or the foot before asking for the movement.

Guide through the full range

For rotation especially — where clients genuinely cannot feel how little they're moving — gently placing hands on the hips and physically guiding the correct rotation lets them feel the target. This is often more effective than any amount of verbal or visual cueing.

Use alongside other cues

Physical feedback works best in combination with vocal and visual cues — not as a replacement for them. Touch alongside a clear verbal cue creates a multi-sensory input that's far more powerful than any single channel alone.

Build trust first

Physical feedback is most effective once a client trusts you. Invest in the relationship — learn their name, remember their challenges, celebrate their progress. When trust is there, a light touch from you carries enormous weight.

Putting It All Together

Combining the Three Techniques

The most effective cueing moments combine all three techniques simultaneously. Here's what each one is best for — and what happens when you bring them together.

Vocal alone

Good for corrections across the room, group energy management, and counting rhythm. Works well when clients are moving well and just need a reminder.

Mirroring alone

Ideal for new movements, confused clients, OFF periods, or when a client needs to reset. Get in front of them and lead them back.

Physical alone

A light tap for body awareness, or a guided hip rotation for a client who genuinely can't feel their range. Always with consent.

All three combined

Most powerful. You're in front of them (mirror), guiding their hip (physical), while saying "rotate — BIG — yes, like that!" (vocal). Multi-sensory input creates multi-sensory change.

Coach's Corner

Your cueing will get better every class. Pay attention to what works for each individual — some clients respond most to your voice, some need to see you, some need to feel it. Over time you'll develop an instinct for which tool to reach for and when.

Key takeaways

  • As Parkinson's progresses, effective cueing becomes more critical — Vocal, Mirroring, and Physical Feedback are your three essential tools.
  • Vocal cueing depends on clarity, repetition, and contagious positive energy; keep cues short and punchy, and centre yourself before class.
  • Mirroring means moving opposite to the client, directly in front of them, at the biggest possible amplitude — especially for your most affected clients.
  • Physical feedback can unlock movement through touch, but consent is non-negotiable: ask, confirm every time, and respect a no immediately.
  • The three techniques are most powerful combined — multi-sensory input creates multi-sensory change, and your instinct for which to use will grow every class.

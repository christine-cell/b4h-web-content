Lesson 1 · Parkinson's SymptomsLeçon 1 · Les symptômes de la maladie de Parkinson

11 min read Updated 2026

Understanding how Parkinson's shows up in the body — and what it means for how you run your classes — is the foundation of safe, effective, transformative instruction. This lesson walks through the primary motor, secondary motor, and non-motor symptoms of Parkinson's, then lets you rehearse them with flashcards and a knowledge check.

What you'll learn

  • Recognise the primary motor symptoms of Parkinson's — tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability — and dyskinesia as a medication side effect.
  • Identify the secondary motor symptoms, including several that significantly raise fall risk.
  • Understand the often-invisible non-motor symptoms that affect a client's ability to participate.
  • Apply coach-facing strategies for each symptom so your classes stay safe, empathetic, and effective.

Section 01

Primary Motor Symptoms

These are the hallmark symptoms most people associate with Parkinson's. Open any card to see what it means for your classes.

Shaking of one body part or multiple, typically more pronounced on one side of the body. Often most visible at rest and may reduce during purposeful movement.

Coach's Corner

Don't be alarmed by tremors during class — they often decrease once a client begins moving. Never comment on or draw attention to a client's tremor in front of others.

Stiffness in some or multiple areas of the body. Most prominent in the thoracic/core region — the chest, upper back, and trunk.

Coach's Corner

This is why warm-up and cool-down are non-negotiable. Stiffness is often worse in the morning — plan your session intensity accordingly. Rotational movements help combat thoracic rigidity.

Slow movements, especially during the 'off' phase (when medication is wearing off). The brain has difficulty initiating and executing movement at normal speed.

Coach's Corner

Emphasize BIG movements throughout every class — big punches, big steps, big arm swings. This trains clients to move with intention at home. Don't rush them; give verbal cues like "BIG and STRONG."

Poor posture (hunched over, rounded shoulders) and poor balance. The body's automatic balance-correcting reflexes become impaired.

Coach's Corner

Balance work should be built into every session. Always have a chair or bar available. Watch for forward lean — cue clients to "stand tall" and "shoulders back" constantly.

Involuntary, erratic movements of the face, arms, trunk, and legs. Typically occurs at peak medication effect of levodopa — not a worsening of PD itself. Some clients actually prefer dyskinesia over feeling rigid and 'off.'

Coach's Corner

Stress and cognitive multitasking can worsen dyskinesia. Keep your cueing calm and clear. If a client is having a high-dyskinesia day, simplify the exercises and reduce cognitive demands.

Section 02

Secondary Motor Symptoms

Safety

Fall Risk Alert: Several secondary motor symptoms — particularly shuffling gait, stooped posture, and freezing — significantly increase fall risk. Always ensure stations have chairs nearby and be prepared to offer a hand or arm for support.

Forward-leaning position causes the centre of gravity to shift forward, resulting in a greater risk of falling. Back pain and overall body stiffness are common companions.

Coach's Corner

Boxing naturally fights stooped posture — guard position requires upright spine and raised arms. Use this as a teaching moment: "This is your boxing posture — this is also your best posture."

Small, hesitant steps can lead to shuffling gait. Clients tend to walk with their feet very close together, severely compromising balance and increasing fall risk. Freezing episodes (sudden inability to move) can occur especially at doorways or when changing direction.

Coach's Corner

Marching in place, high knees, and exaggerated stepping drills directly target this. For freezing, teach clients the trick of stepping over an imaginary line or a visual target on the floor.

Often one side of the body is more affected and you can notice a decreased arm swing when the client is walking. This is an early and often subtle sign.

Coach's Corner

This is literally what boxing addresses head-on. Cross-body punching movements directly train reciprocal arm swing. Point out improvements to clients — "Look how much more your arms are swinging today!"

Clients often have a flat, expressionless face — not because they're unengaged, but because facial muscle stiffness makes it hard to form expressions. Also called "hypomimia."

Coach's Corner

Don't mistake a blank expression for disinterest or unhappiness. Check in verbally — "How are you feeling today?" Many clients are having a great time even when their face doesn't show it. Facial exercises during warm-up help!

Very small handwriting — often one of the first signs of Parkinson's before motor symptoms become obvious. The same bradykinesia that slows movement also shrinks handwriting over time.

Coach's Corner

If clients fill out paperwork or waivers, allow plenty of time and don't comment on handwriting size. This can be a sensitive topic for some clients who notice this as an early symptom.

Stiffness is often worst in the morning — before medication kicks in and before the body has had a chance to move. Rolling over and sitting up can be significant challenges.

Coach's Corner

Warm-up and cool-down stretches are especially important — never skip them! If turning over is a main complaint, teach clients a specific turning-over exercise they can practice at home daily.

Section 03

Non-Motor Symptoms

These symptoms are often invisible but can significantly impact a client's ability to participate. Open any card to reveal instructor tips.

Vocal cord muscle thinning causes a soft, hoarse voice. A gap forms between cords, reducing volume and clarity as the disease progresses.

Coach's Corner

Always face the client when giving instructions. Never talk over music when coaching someone with voice difficulties. Encourage them to speak loudly during class — it's great vocal exercise!

Often in the feet but can present in different areas of the body. These cramps (dystonias) can be sudden and quite severe.

Coach's Corner

If a client stops suddenly or appears uncomfortable, ask gently if they're having a cramp. Know your modification for that station. Never push through visible pain.

Disrupted sleep worsens all other symptoms. Excessive daytime sleepiness, hallucinations, and vivid dreams can occur as the disease progresses.

Coach's Corner

If a client seems unusually fatigued, reduce intensity without making it obvious. Regular exercise actually improves sleep quality in PD — another benefit to share with clients!

Social isolation, increased anxiety, fear, and feeling overwhelmed are very common. Anxiety can directly exacerbate motor symptoms.

Coach's Corner

Community is medicine. The social environment of your class may be as therapeutic as the exercise itself. Notice if a regular client stops coming — reach out. Consistency in attendance is a key health indicator.

Slowness in memory and thinking affects many with PD. Clients may take longer processing directions or not clearly understand an exercise cue.

Coach's Corner

Break down exercises into simple, one-step cues. Demonstrate before explaining. Be patient — repeat instructions calmly and without frustration. Dual-tasking (moving + counting + thinking) is actually excellent cognitive exercise!

Very common in PD — either from the disease itself or medication. Getting up from the floor too quickly can cause dangerous dizziness or fainting.

Coach's Corner

Always place a chair at every station that involves going to the ground. Teach clients to get up slowly — pause seated, then stand. Know your ground-to-stand modifications for every exercise.

Oily or scaly skin can become dry and sensitive. Some clients may not be able to go barefoot in the gym.

Coach's Corner

Recommend non-slip socks or indoor shoes if needed. Never require bare feet for balance exercises — safety first, always.

Very common and often an early non-motor sign of PD. Digestive health is now understood to be a meaningful part of the PD picture.

Coach's Corner

Exercise promotes gut motility — another benefit of your program! Encourage clients to work with their healthcare team on digestive health. You can mention it as something worth discussing with their doctor.

Symptom Flashcards

Tremor

Bradykinesia

Slowness of movement, especially during medication "off" phases. Instructor note: Always cue BIG, exaggerated movements. This trains neurological amplitude and carries over to daily life.

Rigidity

Muscle stiffness, most prominent in the thoracic/core. Instructor note: Warm-up is non-negotiable. Stiffness is worst in the morning. Rotational movements help.

Postural Instability

Poor posture and balance from impaired righting reflexes. Instructor note: Boxing guard position naturally combats this. Cue "stand tall" constantly.

Dyskinesia

Involuntary erratic movements from peak levodopa effect. Instructor note: Stress worsens it. Keep cueing calm and exercises simple on high-dyskinesia days.

Shuffling Gait

Small, hesitant steps with feet close together. High fall risk. Instructor note: High knees and marching drills target this directly.

Hypomimia

Reduced facial expression from facial muscle stiffness. Instructor note: Never mistake a blank face for disengagement. Check in verbally.

Micrographia

Tap to flip

Very small handwriting — often an early PD sign. Instructor note: Give plenty of time for paperwork and never comment on handwriting size.

Orthostatic Hypotension

Tap to flip

Blood pressure drops when moving from floor to standing. Instructor note: Always have a chair at every floor station. Teach slow, staged getting-up.

Cognitive Changes

Slowness in memory, thinking, and processing. Instructor note: Use simple one-step cues, demonstrate before explaining. Dual-tasking is great cognitive exercise!

Key takeaways

  • Primary motor symptoms — tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability — are the hallmarks of PD; dyskinesia is a levodopa side effect, not a worsening of the disease.
  • Rigidity and bradykinesia are typically worst in the morning before medication takes effect, so a thorough warm-up is the most important thing you can do.
  • Shuffling gait, stooped posture, and freezing raise fall risk — keep chairs nearby and be ready to offer support.
  • A flat face (hypomimia) is muscle stiffness, not disinterest; check in verbally and treat clients normally.
  • Low blood pressure is common — always have a chair at floor stations and teach slow, staged getting-up.
  • Cueing BIG, intentional movements retrains the nervous system to counteract bradykinesia and carries over into daily life.

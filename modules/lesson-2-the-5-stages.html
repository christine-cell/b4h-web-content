01 — The Hoehn & Yahr Scale

Who Were Hoehn & Yahr?

Dr. Margaret Hoehn and Dr. Melvin Yahr developed this five-stage scale to classify the progression of Parkinson's Disease. Stage 1 represents the mildest form, while Stage 5 is the most advanced. Importantly, progression is not always linear — stages can be skipped (e.g. jumping from Stage 1 to Stage 3), and the time spent at each stage varies greatly from person to person.

1 Mild
2 Bilateral
3 Balance
4 Severe
5 Advanced
1
Mild — One Side Only
Unilateral symptoms · Still highly independent
▼

Clinical Picture

  • Tremor, muscle stiffness, slowness, and posture issues on one side of the body only
  • Balance problems may begin to appear
  • Symptoms may be subtle — sometimes newly diagnosed

What You'll See in Class

  • High functioning — may not look like they have PD
  • Possibly newly diagnosed and in denial
  • No cognitive concerns at this stage
  • Minimal on/off medication periods
  • Can make modifications on their own
  • Often competitive — watch for "weekend warrior" tendencies
  • May still be working full time
  • Target 80% of max heart rate for best results

🥊 Instructor Note: Stage 1 clients are your best advocates for the program — they often show the most dramatic improvements. Channel their competitiveness positively but watch that they don't push too hard and injure themselves. Some may not want others to know about their diagnosis — be discreet.

2
Bilateral — Both Sides Affected
Symptoms now visible on both sides · More modifications needed
▼

Clinical Picture

  • Disease has spread to both sides of the body
  • Minor swallowing and talking difficulties may appear
  • Facial masking (loss of expression) becomes noticeable
  • Client remains independent in daily activities

What You'll See in Class

  • Symptoms are more visible to others
  • Posture, gait, and balance more affected
  • Will need more exercise modifications
  • Work stress may trigger symptom flare-ups
  • Voice may be affected — low tone, softer

🥊 Instructor Note: Speak clearly and face the client when giving instructions. Begin introducing seated or supported modifications alongside standing work. Acknowledge their hard work — symptoms becoming visible can be emotionally challenging.

3
Balance Loss — Still Independent
Mid-stage · Balance issues emerge · Still self-sufficient
▼

Clinical Picture

  • Same symptoms as Stage 2 but potentially more severe
  • Balance problems now clearly present — first time balance becomes a significant safety concern
  • Person is still independent in daily life

What You'll See in Class

  • Movements are much slower
  • Cognitive impairments becoming noticeable
  • Non-verbal communication issues
  • Signs of de-conditioning
  • Will need more time and patience with instructions

🥊 Instructor Note: Always have a chair available. Break instructions into single steps — demonstrate first, then explain. Consider pairing this client near a wall or railing. De-conditioning is reversible through consistent attendance — be encouraging.

4
Severe — Assistance Required
Later stage · Significant limitations · 1-on-1 often needed
▼

Clinical Picture

  • Increasing disability in daily life
  • Needs help with some or all activities of daily living
  • Walking limitations — may use a walker or require assistance
  • Significant cognitive impairments present

What You'll See in Class

  • Later stage Parkinson's presentation
  • Significant walking limitations
  • Cognitive impairments require extra patience
  • One-on-one assistance often required throughout class
  • All exercises will need modification

🥊 Instructor Note: Consider whether a volunteer or co-instructor can support this client 1-on-1. Focus on seated boxing, chair-based movements, and upper body work. The emotional and social value of attending class is enormous at this stage — presence matters as much as performance.

5
Advanced — Full Assistance
Wheelchair or bed-bound · Total care required
▼

Clinical Picture

  • Person is confined to a wheelchair or bed
  • Requires total assistance for all daily activities
  • Most severe presentation of Parkinson's

What You'll See in Class

  • Fully wheelchair bound or possibly bedridden
  • All participation is adapted — upper body focus
  • Caregiver or family member typically present
  • Engagement and social connection remain meaningful

🥊 Instructor Note: With the right adaptations, Stage 5 clients can still meaningfully participate in chair-based boxing. Upper body punching movements, music, and community connection all have real value. Coordinate closely with caregivers and their healthcare team.

02 — B4H Class Levels

At Boxing 4 Health we offer four levels of programming designed to meet clients exactly where they are. As our locations grow, we conduct class assessments to ensure everyone is in the most suitable class for their mobility, cognition, and individual needs.

Class placement is handled with care. We understand that requesting a class change can feel sensitive. All placement decisions are made based on careful assessment of mobility, cognition, and individual needs — with the client's best experience always as the top priority. If a change is recommended, clients are encouraged to trust the process. And if they feel they need to switch, they can always contact the office to discuss options with a manager.

04 — Knowledge Check

QUESTION 01 / 04 A new client is highly energetic, shows tremor only on one side, has no balance issues, and seems almost competitive in class. Which stage are they most likely in?
AStage 1 — mild, unilateral symptoms
BStage 2 — bilateral symptoms
CStage 3 — balance issues present
DStage 4 — needs assistance
✓ Correct! Stage 1 is characterized by one-sided symptoms, high functioning, no cognitive concerns, and often a competitive mindset. These clients are doing well — keep the energy high and watch for overexertion.
This description fits Stage 1 — unilateral (one-sided) symptoms, high functioning, no balance or cognitive concerns, and a competitive spirit are all classic Stage 1 indicators.
QUESTION 02 / 04 According to Hoehn & Yahr, at which stage do balance problems first become a significant, noticeable concern?
AStage 1
BStage 2
CStage 3
DStage 4
✓ Correct! Stage 3 is the turning point for balance — the client is still independent but balance problems are now clearly present and must be factored into every exercise. A chair should always be within reach from this stage on.
Balance problems become significant at Stage 3. While minor balance concerns may exist before, Stage 3 is when they become a real safety consideration in class — even though the client is still living independently.
QUESTION 03 / 04 True or False: A person with Parkinson's must always progress through every stage in order (1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 5).
ATrue — progression always follows the sequence
BFalse — stages can be skipped and time at each stage varies greatly
✓ Correct! Progression is not always linear. Skipping stages (e.g. going from Stage 1 to Stage 3) is not uncommon. Time spent at each stage also varies widely — some people stay at Stage 1 for many years, others progress faster.
False — Parkinson's progression is not always linear. Stages can be skipped entirely, and the time spent at each stage varies dramatically from person to person. Never assume a client will follow a predictable path.
QUESTION 04 / 04 A Stage 4 client arrives to your class. What is the MOST important adjustment to make?
AAsk them to sit and watch rather than participate
BRun the class exactly as normal — treat everyone the same
CEnsure 1-on-1 assistance is available and all exercises are adapted — but keep them participating
DRefer them to a physiotherapist instead
✓ Perfect answer. Stage 4 clients need hands-on support and fully adapted exercises — but participation is still absolutely the goal. The social connection, movement, and sense of achievement matter enormously at this stage.
Stage 4 clients need 1-on-1 assistance and modified exercises — but they should absolutely still participate. Asking them to sit out or redirecting them elsewhere misses the entire point of what Boxing 4 Health offers.

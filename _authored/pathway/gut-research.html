You may already know that Parkinson’s affects movement — but did you know it also has a deep connection to your gut? Researchers are discovering that the health of your digestive system plays a much bigger role in Parkinson’s than anyone realized. Here is what the science says, in plain language.

The gut–brain connection: why your gut matters

Your gut and your brain are in constant communication through a pathway researchers call the gut–brain axis. Think of it as a two-way highway between your digestive system and your brain, sending signals back and forth all day long.

In Parkinson’s, this communication system can be disrupted. Research has found that people with Parkinson’s often have changes in their gut microbiome — the trillions of bacteria that live in your digestive tract. These changes can affect inflammation, brain health, and many of the non-motor symptoms you may experience.

From the research

“The gut microbiota is strongly associated with Parkinson’s disease. Intestinal issues often occur before motor symptoms appear — suggesting the gut may play a role early in the disease process.” — Translational Neurodegeneration, 2023

One protein central to Parkinson’s — called alpha-synuclein — is believed to potentially begin accumulating in the gut and travel to the brain via the vagus nerve (the main nerve connecting gut and brain). This is an active and exciting area of research.

Constipation: a common challenge in Parkinson’s

Constipation is one of the most common non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s, and it is often under-discussed. Research shows that gut movement (motility) is frequently slowed in people with Parkinson’s, partly because dopamine — the same brain chemical affected by Parkinson’s — also plays a role in regulating the digestive system.

Good news

Studies show that dietary changes can meaningfully improve constipation in people with Parkinson’s — sometimes within just a few weeks.

What the studies found

Study 1

Mediterranean diet: 5 weeks to fewer symptoms

What researchers did: Scientists at the University of Florida followed people with Parkinson’s who ate a Mediterranean diet for just 5 weeks. They measured constipation scores, gut bacteria and other digestive markers before and after.

What they found:

  • Constipation scores significantly improved after 5 weeks.
  • Gut bacteria changed in helpful ways — beneficial bacteria increased, while potentially harmful strains decreased.
  • Participants also experienced weight loss.
  • The diet was shown to be realistic and achievable for people with Parkinson’s.

Key takeaway: Just 5 weeks of eating a Mediterranean diet made a measurable difference in gut health and constipation for people living with Parkinson’s.

Source: Rusch et al., Frontiers in Neurology, December 2021 — University of Florida, Norman Fixel Institute for Neurological Diseases. Read the abstract: 5 weeks of Mediterranean diet improved constipation in PD and changed the intestinal microbiome.

Study 2

A larger, stronger study confirms the results (2024)

Because the original 2021 study was small, the same research team returned with a larger, more rigorous randomized controlled trial — the gold standard of medical research. Published in September 2024, it compared people following a Mediterranean diet with a standard-care group over 8 weeks.

What they found:

  • Both groups saw reduced constipation — but the Mediterranean diet group ate significantly more dietary fibre.
  • The Mediterranean diet group showed lower intestinal inflammation (measured by a marker called calprotectin).
  • This suggests the Mediterranean diet does more than just relieve symptoms — it may actually reduce inflammation in the gut.

Why does gut inflammation matter?

Chronic (long-lasting) inflammation in the gut is thought to contribute to Parkinson’s disease progression. Reducing it through diet is a simple, safe and powerful tool.

Source: Rusch et al., Nutrients, September 2024 — University of Florida, Norman Fixel Institute for Neurological Diseases.

Study 3

A review of all the evidence (2024)

A 2024 review from University College London looked at all available studies on the Mediterranean diet, the gut microbiome and Parkinson’s together. Reviewing both observational studies (over 1,200 participants) and clinical trials, they found consistent patterns across the research:

  • Following the Mediterranean diet closely was linked to helpful changes in gut bacteria — especially an increase in bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids (which protect the gut lining).
  • Improvements were seen in constipation, thinking skills (cognitive function) and indigestion.
  • No significant effect on motor symptoms was found — but researchers note more and longer studies are needed.

Source: Seelarbokus et al., Nutrients, July 2024 — University College London, UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology.

What does the Mediterranean diet actually look like?

The good news: this is not a restrictive or complicated diet. It focuses on whole, natural foods that are colourful, flavourful and satisfying.

Eat plenty of

  • Vegetables — as many colours as possible
  • Fresh fruit
  • Whole grains (oats, brown rice, quinoa)
  • Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, beans)
  • Nuts and seeds
  • Olive oil as your main fat
  • Fish, especially fatty fish (salmon, sardines)
  • Herbs and spices for flavour

Limit or reduce

  • Ultra-processed foods
  • Fast food and fried foods
  • Sugary drinks and sweets
  • Red meat (occasional is fine)
  • Highly refined white bread and pasta
  • Packaged snack foods

Other promising research areas

Beyond diet, researchers are exploring other ways to support gut health in Parkinson’s. These are still emerging, but the early findings are encouraging:

Probiotics and prebiotics

Beneficial bacteria supplements may help improve bowel motility and reduce gut inflammation.

Resistant starch

A type of fibre found in foods like cooled potatoes, unripe bananas and oats. It has been studied in a clinical trial (RESISTA-PD) for its effects on gut bacteria in Parkinson’s.

Fecal microbiome transplant (FMT)

Transferring healthy gut bacteria from a donor into a patient’s gut. Early studies show promise for constipation relief, though this is still experimental.

Bottom line: Diet remains the most practical, safe and evidence-supported tool available right now for supporting gut health in Parkinson’s.

What you can start doing today

  • Add one more vegetable to your plate at lunch and dinner.
  • Swap refined bread or pasta for a whole-grain version.
  • Add a handful of nuts or seeds as a daily snack.
  • Use olive oil instead of butter when cooking.
  • Aim for fish twice a week — salmon, sardines or trout are great choices.
  • Drink water steadily throughout the day to support bowel regularity.
  • Add legumes (lentil soup, chickpea salad) to your weekly meals.

You do not need to overhaul your entire diet overnight. Small, consistent changes add up. Research shows that even a short-term improvement in eating patterns can produce meaningful changes in your gut health.

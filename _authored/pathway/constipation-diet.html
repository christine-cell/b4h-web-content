Why this matters

Constipation in Parkinson’s: more than just a nuisance

When we think about Parkinson’s disease, we often focus on tremors and movement. But the gut tells a story too — and it often starts years before motor symptoms appear. Up to 80% of people with Parkinson’s experience constipation, which can worsen quality of life, affect how medications are absorbed, and may even be connected to disease progression.

Researchers at the University of Florida asked a simple but powerful question: could eating a Mediterranean diet improve gut symptoms and change the microbiome (the bacteria living in your gut) in people with Parkinson’s?

The answer — even from this small pilot study — was encouraging. In just 5 weeks, participants who followed a Mediterranean diet had fewer constipation symptoms and notable shifts in their gut bacteria.

The diet

What is the Mediterranean diet?

Think of it as eating like you live near the Greek coast. It’s rich in plants, healthy fats and whole foods — and naturally high in the fibre and anti-inflammatory nutrients that your gut loves.

The research

What the study found

Eight people with Parkinson’s followed a Mediterranean diet for 5 weeks, guided by the study’s dietitians. Their live-in partners served as healthy comparison “controls.” Here’s what happened:

Constipation symptoms improved

Participants reported fewer constipation symptoms — including less straining, fewer hard stools, and less of a feeling that they hadn’t fully emptied — after just 5 weeks on the diet.

The gut microbiome shifted

Stool tests showed real changes in the types and balance of bacteria living in the gut. Notably, there were increases in bacteria linked to fibre fermentation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production — chemicals that are helpful for gut health and may even support brain health.

Fibre intake jumped

Participants significantly increased their daily fibre by following the Mediterranean diet. This matters because most people with Parkinson’s don’t eat enough fibre, which directly affects bowel regularity.

“Leaky gut” was measured

The researchers also checked markers of intestinal permeability (“leaky gut”). The pilot was too small to draw firm conclusions, but tracking these markers shows how seriously gut health is being studied in Parkinson’s research.

The gut–brain connection

Scientists increasingly believe that Parkinson’s may actually begin in the gut — specifically with the misfolding of a protein called alpha-synuclein, which may travel from the gut up to the brain via the vagus nerve (the main nerve connecting gut and brain). A disrupted gut microbiome (called dysbiosis) can trigger inflammation and may contribute to disease progression.

  1. Poor diet

    A diet low in fibre and high in processed foods sets the stage.

  2. Gut dysbiosis

    The balance of gut bacteria is disrupted.

  3. Inflammation

    Dysbiosis can trigger inflammation in the gut.

  4. More alpha-synuclein

    Misfolded alpha-synuclein may build up and travel along the vagus nerve.

  5. Brain impact

    The process may reach the brain and contribute to disease progression.

Please note

This was a pilot study with a small number of people (8). The results are promising but should be seen as a first look. A later, larger randomized controlled trial by the same team confirmed key benefits, including less gut inflammation.

Talk to your care team

This lesson is for education only and isn’t medical advice. Check with your doctor, neurologist or pharmacist before starting a supplement or making a big change to your diet or medication.

This is especially important with Parkinson’s medication — for example, high-protein meals can affect how levodopa is absorbed.

Check in on your gut

These questions have no right or wrong answer. Open each one, pick the answer that fits you best, and read what it may mean.

  • Every 1–2 days — regular as clockwork: That’s a healthy frequency! Most guidelines consider 3 times a day to 3 times a week as the normal range. Staying well hydrated and eating fibre helps keep things moving.
  • Every 3 days — pretty normal for me: Every 3 days is on the lower end of normal, but may be fine for some people. If it comes with straining or hard stools, it’s worth mentioning to your doctor.
  • Every 4–5 days or less often: Going less often than every 3 days may mean constipation, especially if it’s difficult. Consider increasing your fibre and fluids.
  • Rarely — sometimes a week or more: Going a week or more between bowel movements is a concern and is considered constipation. It can affect how your medication is absorbed and your quality of life with Parkinson’s. Please speak with your care team.
  • Easy and complete — no straining: Excellent! Comfortable, easy bowel movements are a sign of good gut health. Keep up the fibre and water!
  • Occasionally hard but usually fine: Straining now and then can be normal, but if it’s happening more often, try adding more fibre-rich foods like vegetables, legumes and whole grains.
  • I often have to strain a lot: Frequent straining is a key sign of constipation, and it can make other conditions worse. A Mediterranean-style diet rich in fibre may help — and it’s worth discussing with your care team.
  • I often feel like I haven’t fully emptied: This feeling is one of the hallmark symptoms of constipation and is very common in Parkinson’s. On its own, it’s worth a conversation with your doctor.
  • 5 or more — I love my produce: Fantastic! Eating lots of fruit and vegetables is a cornerstone of the Mediterranean diet, and gives you the fibre, antioxidants and prebiotics (food for good gut bacteria) that support a healthy gut.
  • 3–4 servings most days: Good effort! Aim to bump that up to 5 or more by adding an extra handful of vegetables at meals, or fruit as a snack. Small changes add up.
  • 1–2 servings — I could do better: Most people benefit from at least 5 servings of fruit and vegetables a day. Try a salad at lunch, roasted veggies at dinner, or a banana at breakfast. Your gut bacteria will thank you!
  • Rarely or almost never: Very low fruit and vegetable intake is a key risk factor for constipation and a less diverse gut. Start small — even one extra serving a day can make a difference over time.
  • 6–8 glasses (1.5–2 litres) — I stay well hydrated: Excellent! Water works with fibre to keep stool soft and moving. This is especially important with Parkinson’s, where constipation is already a big challenge.
  • About 4–5 glasses: Not bad, but try to reach 6–8 glasses a day. Carry a water bottle, set reminders, or try herbal teas.
  • 2–3 glasses — I often forget: Low fluid intake is a major cause of constipation. Even with good fibre, without enough water stool can become hard and difficult to pass. Try to increase gradually.
  • Barely 1–2 glasses: Very low hydration is a serious risk factor for constipation. Try starting your day with a glass of water and keeping one nearby at all times.

Key takeaways

  • Constipation is one of the most common non-motor symptoms of Parkinson’s, and it deserves attention — not silence.
  • A Mediterranean diet — high in fibre, vegetables and healthy fats — may meaningfully reduce constipation in people with Parkinson’s.
  • Your gut bacteria can change with what you eat, and changes can happen within weeks.
  • Short-chain fatty acids made by gut bacteria may help protect the brain — another reason diet matters.
  • The pilot study was small (8 people), but a later randomized controlled trial by the same team confirmed key benefits, including less gut inflammation.
  • Eating more fruit, vegetables, legumes and whole grains is a low-risk, high-reward step you can take.

Source: Rusch et al. (2021). Mediterranean Diet Adherence in People With Parkinson’s Disease Reduces Constipation Symptoms and Changes Fecal Microbiota After a 5-Week Single-Arm Pilot Study. Frontiers in Neurology, 12, 794640.

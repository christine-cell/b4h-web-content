Why this study is different

Moving beyond food surveys — into our DNA

For years, researchers have noticed a pattern: people who eat and drink more dairy seem to have a higher risk of developing Parkinson’s. But critics rightly asked: could this just be coincidence? Could people who were already developing Parkinson’s in its early, silent stages be changing their diet? Could other lifestyle factors explain the link?

This 2022 study tackled those questions head-on using Mendelian randomization — a powerful genetic technique that uses naturally occurring DNA differences as a “natural experiment” to test whether a link is truly cause-and-effect, not just something that happens together.

The researchers used data from the huge COURAGE-PD Consortium, combining 23 international studies with nearly 10,000 people with Parkinson’s and over 8,000 healthy people for comparison — one of the largest and most careful analyses of this question ever done.

What is Mendelian randomization?

Think of it as a genetic lottery. Some people are born with a version of the lactase gene that makes them naturally inclined to have more dairy throughout their lives. Because this gene version is handed out at random at conception — before any disease develops — it can’t be influenced by lifestyle choices or early disease symptoms. By comparing Parkinson’s rates in people with and without this gene version, researchers can test cause-and-effect far more rigorously than any food survey.

The research

What the study found

A genetic tendency toward dairy was linked to higher risk

People genetically inclined to have more dairy had 70% higher odds of developing Parkinson’s (odds ratio 1.70 for each extra serving per day). This held up even after accounting for other factors — a key strength of the Mendelian randomization approach.

The effect was strongest in men

When the data were split by sex, the link was far stronger in men, who showed a 2.5-fold increase in Parkinson’s risk (odds ratio 2.50). The difference between men and women was statistically significant, which suggests a possible sex-specific biological cause worth studying further.

Consistent across 23 international studies

The finding held across a wide range of people from Europe, Asia, Australia and South Africa. This consistency across cultures is especially convincing and hard to explain by other factors alone.

Cause, not just coincidence

This design tackles two big concerns about earlier studies: (1) reverse causation — the idea that early Parkinson’s symptoms might change what people eat, and (2) confounding — the idea that dairy lovers might share other risk factors. The genetic approach effectively ruled out both.

Not all dairy is equal

What earlier studies tell us about specific products

This genetic study looked at dairy intake as a whole. Earlier large observational studies (like the 129,346-person Harvard study) looked at different dairy products separately. The picture that emerges is nuanced:

Good to know

The product-by-product findings come from observational studies and should be read with caution. The 2022 genetic study looked at total dairy intake.

Why might dairy be linked to Parkinson’s?

Researchers have suggested several possible biological pathways. No single explanation has been confirmed, but the leading theories are:

Contaminants

Pesticide and environmental residues in dairy (such as organochlorines), which have been found in higher amounts in the brains of people with Parkinson’s.

Lower uric acid

Dairy lowers uric acid levels. Uric acid is a natural antioxidant thought to help protect the brain.

Gut microbiome changes

Dairy-related changes to your gut bacteria may affect the gut–brain connection.

Together, these may make nerve cells in the brain more vulnerable. Because low-fat dairy (not full-fat) shows a stronger signal, some researchers think certain fat-soluble protective compounds in full-fat products may partly offset the risk.

Important note

This study shows a link and supports cause-and-effect through a genetic method, but no single food causes or prevents Parkinson’s. The absolute risk remains low. This information is for education only.

Reflect on your dairy habits

These questions have no right or wrong answer. Open each one, find the answer that fits you, and read what it may mean for you.

  • 3 or more servings — milk, yogurt, cheese daily: This is the level of dairy linked to the highest risk in several studies. With this research in hand, it may be worth talking with your doctor about whether cutting back on dairy is right for you.
  • 1–2 servings most days: This is moderate. The research shows risk rising as intake rises, so paying attention to the type and amount of dairy you have is a reasonable approach.
  • Less than 1 serving a day: Based on current evidence, low dairy intake puts you in the lower-risk group. Keep making informed choices!
  • I have already cut back on or cut out dairy: You’ve already taken a proactive step based on the evidence. Keep an eye on new research and talk it over with your care team.
  • Plant-based milks (oat, almond, soy): Great choice! Plant-based milks have come a long way in taste and nutrition. Look for calcium-fortified options so you still get the calcium you need. Oat milk is especially good for cooking and coffee!
  • Having less, but keeping some dairy: A reasonable middle ground! Based on current evidence, even modest cuts to low-fat dairy may be worthwhile. Paying attention to the type (choosing full-fat if you do have dairy) may also matter.
  • I’m not ready to change my dairy habits yet: That’s completely valid — this is your decision to make, with full information. The research is compelling, but it doesn’t mean everyone must change. Start by learning more and talking with your care team.
  • I’d like to talk with my doctor first: That’s the wisest approach! Your doctor can help you weigh the evidence in light of your own health history, nutrition needs and other risk factors.
  • High — I have dairy with most meals: Given this research, it may be worth reflecting on your intake — especially low-fat products like skim milk. A conversation with your doctor or dietitian can help you make choices that fit your lifestyle and health goals.
  • Moderate — a few times a week: Moderate intake puts you in a middle-risk range. Staying informed and paying attention to the type of dairy you have (favouring full-fat if you do have dairy) is a reasonable approach based on current evidence.
  • Low — I rarely have dairy: Low dairy intake matches the patterns linked to lower Parkinson’s risk in the research. Keep making informed choices!
  • None — I’m already dairy-free: You’ve already made a choice that fits the growing body of research. Your proactive approach to your health is something to be proud of!

Key takeaways

  • This 2022 genetic study is among the strongest evidence yet that the dairy–Parkinson’s link may be cause-and-effect, not just coincidence.
  • The risk was highest for men, who showed a 2.5-fold increase — worth discussing with your care team.
  • Earlier evidence suggests low-fat and skim milk carry the strongest signal; full-fat dairy may not carry the same risk.
  • Possible reasons include contaminants, lower uric acid (a natural brain protector) and changes to your gut bacteria.
  • This does not mean dairy causes Parkinson’s in every person — the absolute risk remains low — but it is a food choice you can change if you decide to.
  • Talk over any diet changes with your care team, so your approach fits you.

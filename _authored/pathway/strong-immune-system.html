Introduction

Your immune system is one of your greatest assets

Boosting your immune system plays an important role in staying healthy — and when you live with Parkinson’s, this is especially true. No supplement replaces medication or medical care, but the right nutrition strategies can give your body the upper hand to fight off bugs, reduce inflammation and help you feel your best.

What follows is my carefully chosen list of foundational supplements and habits — the ones I return to again and again with my Parkinson’s clients. Remember: we are all unique. The best mix for each person has to be tailored to them. But these are excellent places to start.

Christine’s tip

“Our challenges don’t define us. Our actions do.” Eating a clean, whole-foods diet with lots of vegetables is going to be the game changer. Adding supplements helps fill in the gaps and buffer your immune system, so your whole body works at its best for you.

Vitamin C

Antioxidant · levodopa absorption · immune support

Vitamin C is a potent antioxidant (a substance that protects your cells from damage), well known for supporting a healthy immune system. With Parkinson’s, it has extra value: it can actually help increase your ability to absorb levodopa. That makes it an essential supplement in my view.

Your body can’t absorb more than 1,000 mg of vitamin C at a time, so it’s best to split your dose through the day. A great strategy is to take it alongside your levodopa. Vitamin C is water-soluble, which means your body simply gets rid of any extra it doesn’t need — making it one of the safest supplements available.

The bowel tolerance test

You can self-test your personal vitamin C needs. Gradually increase your dose until your stools start to become loose — that is your body’s maximum. I have yet to hear of a Parkinson’s client reaching their tolerance level!

The powder form taken with levodopa is especially convenient and effective. Some people may need a little more or less depending on things like body weight, overall health and how much medication they take.

Vitamin D

Brain protection · bone health · immune balance

Vitamin D deficiency (not having enough) is surprisingly common. Your risk goes up a lot if you live farther from the equator, are less active, stay mostly indoors, or cover up with clothing when you’re outside. People with Parkinson’s have a higher-than-average chance of developing skin cancer, so I strongly recommend exploring alternatives to direct sunlight for getting your vitamin D.

Test before you supplement

It’s very important to know your vitamin D level before starting a supplement. Ask your family doctor to check it — it’s a simple blood test. Your baseline result will determine exactly how much you need.

My clients have needed anywhere from 2,000 IU up to 10,000 IU a day to reach healthy levels — a wide range that shows how individual this supplement truly is. Body weight is one of the key factors in finding the right dose.

People with Parkinson’s have a higher risk of falls and fractures, and vitamin D is important for both bone strength and muscle function — two more good reasons to make sure your levels are where they should be.

What the research says — vitamins C & D in Parkinson’s

Could vitamins help treat Parkinson’s?

Brain Sciences · MDPI · 2023

A 2023 review confirmed that oxidative stress (cell damage from unstable molecules) is a central driver of nerve-cell loss in Parkinson’s, and that vitamins with antioxidant properties — including vitamin C — are a promising treatment strategy. Vitamin C is concentrated in nerve-rich areas of the brain and can be carried into the brain by dedicated transport proteins, giving it direct brain-protecting potential.

Key point: vitamin C is concentrated in the brain and may protect it directly.

Vitamin C and levodopa absorption

PMC · Dietary antioxidants & PD · 2020

A peer-reviewed analysis found that vitamin C may improve how well levodopa works by helping your body absorb more of it and helping reduce its harmful side effects. A study in older people with Parkinson’s documented increased levodopa absorption when it was taken with dietary ascorbic acid (vitamin C) — especially relevant for people who absorb their medication poorly.

Key point: vitamin C boosted levodopa absorption in older people with Parkinson’s.

Vitamin D deficiency is very common in Parkinson’s

Nutrients · Pignolo et al. · 2022

A 2022 review confirmed that vitamin D deficiency is significantly more common in people with Parkinson’s than in healthy people or people with Alzheimer’s disease. The vitamin D receptor, and the enzyme that converts vitamin D to its active form, are both highly present in the substantia nigra — the brain area most affected by Parkinson’s — suggesting a direct protective role.

Key point: 55% of people with Parkinson’s have low vitamin D, compared with 36% of healthy people.

Vitamin D3 improved movement and immune response

Scientific Reports · 2025 · Randomized controlled trial

A 2024–2025 double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 51 people with Parkinson’s and low vitamin D found that three months of vitamin D3 supplements produced measurable improvements in motor function and immune response markers compared with placebo. The authors concluded that correcting a deficiency has real benefits beyond bone health alone.

Key point: 3 months of vitamin D3 improved motor function and immune response.

Water

Flushing toxins · constipation relief · energy

You’ve probably been told at some point that you need to drink more water — and this is especially true with Parkinson’s. Toxins build up in your system throughout the day, and the more water you drink, the more efficiently they’re flushed out.

Constipation is one of the most common non-motor symptoms of Parkinson’s, and dehydration is a key contributor. I recommend starting every morning with 1–2 glasses of water. Not only does this help flush toxins, it kick-starts your gut and sets up your digestive system for the day.

Turmeric / curcumin

Anti-inflammatory · gut health · brain support

Have you heard of curcumin? It’s the active ingredient in turmeric — the bright yellow spice common in Asian and Central American cooking. You can add turmeric to your meals for flavour, but you’d need a very large amount to get a therapeutic level of curcumin from food alone. A dedicated supplement is the practical solution.

Curcumin is a powerful antioxidant that helps reduce inflammation in the body, boosts brain power and helps improve digestion. It also has healing properties that support the lining of your blood vessels and keep your heart strong.

Supplement quality matters

Look specifically for a bioavailable form of curcumin (one your body can easily absorb) — such as curcumin combined with piperine (black pepper extract) or in a liposomal formula. Bioavailable forms are absorbed and put to use by your body much more quickly than standard curcumin supplements.

Garlic

Immune boost · fights germs · bone broth partner

I personally love garlic — less keen on the breath, but that’s a small price to pay! Garlic is a wonderful immune-boosting ingredient, especially in bone broths. And there’s a simple technique to get the most out of it.

Prep tip

Cut the garlic and then press or crush it before cooking. This activates a compound called allicin, which gives garlic its powerful germ-fighting and immune-stimulating properties. Loading up on garlic is especially worthwhile in the cooler months.

Adding garlic generously to soups, bone broths and cooked dishes is one of the simplest, most delicious ways to give your immune system a daily boost.

Key takeaways

  • Vitamin C is essential with Parkinson’s — it’s a powerful antioxidant AND may improve levodopa absorption. Split it through the day, ideally with your medication.
  • Get your vitamin D blood level tested before supplementing. Deficiency is very common in Parkinson’s, and the right dose varies widely from person to person.
  • Start every morning with 1–2 glasses of water. Dehydration is a major, often overlooked, contributor to constipation in Parkinson’s.
  • Choose a bioavailable form of curcumin. Adding turmeric to food alone won’t give you a therapeutic dose.
  • Crush or press your garlic before cooking to activate its immune-supporting compounds — and load up in the cooler months.
  • No supplement replaces a clean, whole-foods diet. Supplements fill the gaps; real food is the foundation.
  • We are all unique. Work with your care team to find the right combination and doses for you.

Important

This guide is for education only and doesn’t replace medical advice. Always check with your health care provider or a qualified nutritionist before starting new supplements, especially because of possible interactions with Parkinson’s medications such as levodopa. Individual needs vary a lot.

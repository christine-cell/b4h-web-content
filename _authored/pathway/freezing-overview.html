At a glance: freezing is a motor (movement) symptom that affects walking. It affects up to 80% of people with Parkinson’s and is a major cause of falls. The approach that helps: cueing, exercise and strategy.

Understanding freezing

What is freezing of gait — and why does it happen?

Freezing of gait (FOG) is a sudden, short-lived inability to start or keep walking, even though you intend to move. It feels like your feet are suddenly glued to the floor — often for just a few seconds, sometimes longer. You stay fully conscious and aware, which can make it feel even more frightening and frustrating.

Freezing is not simply “weakness”. It’s a breakdown in the brain’s ability to create the automatic movement patterns needed for walking. In Parkinson’s, the loss of dopamine in the basal ganglia (deep brain areas that control automatic movement) disrupts the brain’s internal “rhythm generator”. Walking, which we normally do on autopilot, suddenly takes enormous conscious effort — and sometimes the system stalls completely.

The brain mechanism

The basal ganglia normally control automatic movement. In Parkinson’s, dopamine loss disrupts this circuit, so starting and continuing to walk become unreliable — especially in complex or dual-task situations (doing two things at once).

What it looks like

Short, shuffling steps; feet that seem glued to the floor; trembling legs with no forward movement; a sudden stop mid-stride. Episodes usually last 1–30 seconds but feel much longer.

When it happens most

Starting to walk, turning, going through doorways, getting close to where you’re going, moving through crowded spaces, or dual-tasking (walking and talking at the same time).

Medication and freezing

Freezing is often worse during “off” periods, when medication is wearing off — but it can also happen during “on” times. This unpredictability is one of the hardest parts of managing freezing.

Why cueing works

When the brain’s internal rhythm generator stalls, outside cues get around the faulty basal ganglia circuits and use other movement pathways — letting the brain take a different route to create movement. That’s why a simple beat, a line on the floor, or even counting can instantly unlock a freeze that feels completely immovable.

Triggers and warning signs

What triggers freezing — and how to recognize it coming

Freezing is not random. Understanding your personal triggers is one of the most powerful tools for managing it. Most triggers fall into three groups: environmental, physical and cognitive (thinking).

Environmental triggers

  • Doorways
  • Turning
  • Getting close to your destination
  • Crowded spaces
  • Narrow hallways
  • Starting to walk
  • Getting up from a chair
  • Obstacles in your path

Physical triggers

  • Medication “off” periods
  • Fatigue
  • Anxiety or stress
  • Moving quickly
  • Changing direction

Cognitive triggers

  • Walking and talking
  • Carrying objects
  • Divided attention
  • Time pressure
  • Fear of falling

Warning signs to watch for

Freezing often has early warning signs: festination (steps getting smaller and faster), a feeling that your feet are “sticking”, more trouble starting turns, or a slight hesitation before doorways. Spotting these signs early gives you time to use a cueing strategy before a full freeze happens — which is always easier than recovering from one mid-episode.

Getting unstuck

Cueing strategies — your toolkit for breaking a freeze

Cueing is the approach with the most evidence behind it for managing freezing. A 2024 review of cueing research confirmed that outside cues consistently improve walking and can greatly reduce how severe freezing is. The key is finding which cues work best for you — and practising them when you’re NOT frozen, so they become automatic.

Auditory cues give the brain a rhythmic beat to lock onto. This gets around the stalled basal ganglia and switches on other movement pathways. Research consistently shows auditory cueing improves step length and walking speed in people with Parkinson’s.

Metronome

A metronome app (set to your normal walking rhythm) gives you a steady beat to walk to. Set it slightly faster than your current pace to encourage full steps. Many phones have free metronome apps.

Music with a beat

Choose music with a clear, steady tempo that matches your natural walking rhythm. Create a dedicated “walking playlist”. The rhythm gives your brain an outside pacing signal to follow.

Counting or commands

Count “1-2-3-GO” or say “left, right, left, right” out loud or in your head. This uses your thinking brain as a workaround when the automatic system stalls. Simple spoken rhythm works remarkably well.

Clapping rhythm

Clap your hands twice, then step forward. The clap creates a rhythmic “launch pad” that can break a freeze. Your care partner can also clap a marching rhythm to help you get moving.

Visual cues give you targets or lines that guide where you put your feet. They get around the automatic system by making walking a conscious, goal-directed action. Evidence shows visual cues — especially laser lines on the floor — can immediately break a freeze.

Floor lines or tape

Put strips of coloured tape on the floor at high-risk spots (kitchen, hallway, bedroom doorway). Step over the lines on purpose. This turns walking into a conscious stepping task instead of an automatic one.

Laser cane or light

Specially designed walking aids shine a laser line on the floor in front of you to step over. Research has shown laser canes greatly reduce how long and how often freezing happens. Some standard walking sticks also have laser attachments.

Target stepping

Focus your eyes on a specific target 1–2 metres ahead (a floor tile, a mark, your care partner’s foot) and aim your next step toward it. This shifts walking from “automatic” to “directed” — a pathway that stays more intact in Parkinson’s.

Side-to-side weight shift

Rock your weight from your left foot to your right foot while picturing each foot lifting. This physical-plus-visual strategy is one of the most reliable ways to break a freeze — the weight shift gets the movement system going again.

Tactile (touch-based) cues use physical sensation to trigger movement. Research in 2023 confirmed that touch-based cues — especially vibration — can reduce how often and how badly freezing happens, especially when turning.

Touch on the shoulder

A gentle touch or tap on the shoulder from a care partner can break a freeze. The sensation interrupts the “loop” the brain is stuck in and gives it a reset signal. Light, rhythmic tapping works especially well.

Knee lift or marching

Lift one knee high on purpose (exaggerate the movement), then the other, as if marching on the spot. This deliberate body-position feedback can re-engage the movement system and restart automatic walking.

Vibration devices

Wearable vibration devices (on the wrists or ankles) give a rhythmic touch signal that can act as a pacing cue. Research published in 2023 showed vibration cues greatly reduced freezing during turning in people with Parkinson’s.

Heel strike focus

Focus on landing heel-first instead of shuffling on the ball of your foot. The feedback from your heel makes each step a deliberate sensory event that gets your movement rhythm going again.

Internal (cognitive) cueing means creating your own movement signals in your mind. These take practice but are always available — no devices needed. They’re especially useful when outside cues aren’t available.

STOP — Breathe — Reset

When you feel a freeze coming: STOP trying to force movement. Take one slow breath. Shift your weight slightly backward. Then start with one deliberate first step. Forcing movement increases anxiety, which makes freezing worse.

Mental imagery

Picture yourself walking normally — your heel striking, your arms swinging, your body moving through space. Then start the movement. Rehearsing in your mind activates some of the same brain pathways as actual movement.

Countdown strategy

Count down from 3: “3 — 2 — 1 — STEP.” This gives you internal timing and takes the pressure off starting to move. The countdown creates a predictable launch moment the brain can prepare for.

Shift to a new task

Sometimes changing how you think about the task breaks the freeze. Instead of “walk to the door”, think “march to the door” or “dance to the door”. Changing the mental label can use different movement circuits.

Step-by-step strategies

What to do when a freeze happens

Having a rehearsed plan matters. When you’re mid-freeze, it’s hard to think clearly — so a practised sequence that comes automatically is key. Here are the most effective step-by-step strategies, in the order to try them.

  1. Step 1 — Stop and don’t panic

    The single most important first step. Panic and force make freezing worse by increasing anxiety and muscle tension. When you feel a freeze:

    • Stop trying to move forward right away.
    • Stand still, feet shoulder-width apart for stability.
    • Take one slow, deliberate breath in through your nose.
    • Breathe out slowly — this calms your nervous system (the parasympathetic, “rest and digest” side) and reduces the anxiety that makes freezing worse.

  2. Step 2 — Shift your weight left to right

    This is one of the most reliably effective strategies. The physical act of shifting your weight gets the movement system going again.

    • Slowly rock your weight onto your left foot.
    • Then shift it onto your right foot.
    • Repeat 2–3 times, making the movement bigger each time.
    • On the next shift, let the unweighted foot lift — and walk.

  3. Step 3 — March on the spot

    If the weight shift alone doesn’t break the freeze, add big knee lifts:

    • Lift your right knee as high as is safe and comfortable.
    • Put your foot down firmly — feel the heel strike.
    • Lift your left knee just as high.
    • Keep marching 4–6 times, then move into walking forward.
    • Keep your arms swinging to reinforce the rhythm.

  4. Step 4 — Add a rhythm or cue

    While marching or shifting your weight, add a sound cue to reinforce the rhythm:

    • Count out loud: “1-2, 1-2” or “left-right-left-right”.
    • Hum or sing a song with a clear marching beat.
    • If you’re wearing earbuds, turn on your metronome or walking playlist.
    • Your care partner can clap a steady rhythm for you to match.

  5. Step 5 — Use a visual target

    Move your attention from “I am frozen” to “I am stepping toward that target”:

    • Pick a specific spot on the floor 1–2 metres ahead.
    • Think only about stepping TO that spot — not about walking in general.
    • Step over an imaginary line if there’s no visual cue.
    • Once you reach the target, pick a new one right away.

  6. Step 6 — Change direction first

    If moving forward is impossible, try moving in a different direction first:

    • Take one step backward (walking backward often gets around freezing).
    • Or take a step sideways — then start moving forward again.
    • The brain often finds it easier to start moving sideways or backward when walking forward is blocked.
    • Only do this when it’s safe — make sure nothing is behind you or beside you.

Exercise and training

Exercises that reduce freezing over time

Research shows that dedicated exercise training — especially activities that combine rhythmic movement, dual-tasking and large, intentional steps — can reduce how often and how badly freezing happens over time. A 2021 meta-analysis (a study that pools the results of many studies) of 41 studies found training had a significant, moderate effect on how severe people felt their freezing was. These aren’t just general fitness exercises — they target what causes freezing.

01 · Rhythm and gait

Tango or rhythmic dance

Tango in particular has been studied a lot in Parkinson’s. The music gives you rhythmic cues, the steps include turning (a major freezing trigger), and the social side reduces anxiety. Even simple tango-step practice at home helps.

02 · Dual-task training

Walk-and-talk practice

Practise walking while talking, counting backward or carrying a light object. This trains your brain to handle divided attention — the exact situation that most often triggers freezing.

03 · Cueing training

Obstacle course practice

Set up a simple obstacle course at home with coloured tape lines to step over, turns to make and a doorway to go through — while playing music. Practising freezing situations safely at home builds resilience and reduces their impact.

04 · Lower-body strength

Exaggerated stepping

Practise high-knee marching, large deliberate steps and landing heel-first during daily exercise. Big, intentional steps use different movement pathways than shuffling and help retrain the brain’s walking patterns.

05 · Balance and turns

Turning practice

Practise turning safely using the “clock turn” method: turn in small steps, clockwise or counter-clockwise, instead of pivoting on one foot. Turns are one of the highest-risk moments for freezing and falls.

06 · Mindful movement

Tai chi

Tai chi improves balance, slows movement to a safe pace and includes deliberate weight shifting — directly targeting several causes of freezing. Research consistently shows better balance and walking in people with Parkinson’s.

Safety first

Practise the hard situations — doorways, turns, starting from a standstill — in a safe, supported environment. Clear your space, and only step backward or sideways when nothing is behind you or beside you.

The key is to practise the difficult situations — doorways, turns, starting from a standstill — in a safe, supported environment, so your brain builds new pathways before you need them in daily life. Consistency matters more than intensity here.

Safety and fall prevention

Staying safe — before, during and after a freeze

Freezing is the leading cause of falls in Parkinson’s. Most falls during freezing happen when people try to force movement against the freeze. Preparation, changes at home and a practised safety plan greatly reduce your risk of falling.

Do

Make changes at home

Put coloured tape strips at known freeze points (doorways, hallway entrances). Remove loose rugs and clutter. Make sure there’s good lighting everywhere. Install grab bars in key spots.

Plan your routes

Find the highest-risk spots in your home and outside. Plan other routes around narrow spaces. Give yourself extra time — time pressure is a major freezing trigger.

Carry a cueing tool

Always carry your favourite cueing tool — a phone with a metronome app, earbuds with your walking playlist, or a walking stick with a laser. Have your toolkit ready before you need it.

Brief your care partner

Make sure your care partner knows your freeze plan. They should offer steady support — no pulling or pushing — and can clap or count a rhythm to help you restart. Practise together regularly.

Don’t

Force your way through a freeze

Trying to force your way through a freeze is the most common cause of falls. The harder you try, the more the system locks up. Stop, breathe and use a strategy instead.

Rush or accept time pressure

Time pressure is a powerful freezing trigger. Build extra time into every outing. Feeling rushed can trigger a freeze on its own — so tell others clearly what you need.

Walk into high-risk places without a plan

If you’re going somewhere with known triggers (crowded malls, busy restaurants), have your cueing strategy ready before you go in. Don’t wait until you’re already freezing to think about what to do.

Avoid walking

Fear of freezing leads many people to stop walking — which quickly makes freezing and other Parkinson’s symptoms worse. The goal is confident management, not avoidance. Regular movement practice protects you.

Talk to your care team

This guide is for education only and doesn’t replace medical advice. Freezing of gait is a complex symptom — work with your neurologist, physiotherapist and Parkinson’s care team to build a plan that fits you. If freezing is seriously affecting your safety, talk to your health care provider about adjusting your medication or seeing a specialist.

Key takeaways

  • Freezing affects up to 80% of people with Parkinson’s and is the leading cause of falls. It’s a brain circuit problem, not a strength or motivation problem.
  • Outside cues — sound, sight and touch — work by getting around the faulty basal ganglia circuits and using other movement pathways. They are the most effective immediate help for freezing.
  • When frozen: STOP, breathe, don’t panic. Shift your weight, march on the spot, add rhythm, pick a visual target — in that order.
  • Find your personal triggers and practise your cueing strategies in those exact places and situations before freezing happens, not just during it.
  • Regular exercise — especially rhythmic, dual-task and turning practice — reduces freezing over time. Tango, tai chi and obstacle course practice are among the best studied.
  • Changes at home (tape strips at doorways, clear routes, grab bars) and a prepared care partner are essential parts of your safety plan.
  • Never try to force your way through a freeze. The harder you push, the worse it gets. Stop, breathe, reset, then use your toolkit.

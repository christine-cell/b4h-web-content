Why it matters

Parkinson’s isn’t just a movement disorder

When most people think of Parkinson’s disease, they picture tremors, stiffness and slowness of movement. But research has firmly established that Parkinson’s is much more than that. Cognitive changes are among the most common and impactful non-motor symptoms (symptoms that aren’t about movement). They can include difficulties with:

  • memory
  • attention
  • processing speed (how quickly you take in and use information)
  • executive function (planning and problem-solving)
  • visuospatial skills (judging space, distance and where things are)

Good to know

A landmark 2021 review in Nature Reviews Disease Primers (Aarsland et al.) confirmed that cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s is now recognized as a defined clinical entity — one that deeply affects quality of life, independence and the load carried by care partners.

Up to 75% of people living with Parkinson’s will develop some degree of cognitive impairment over the course of their disease. Yet cognitive health is one of the areas where proactive, non-drug approaches like cognitive training can make a real difference — and the evidence to support this is growing fast.

The good news? Your brain has a remarkable ability to adapt, rewire and build new connections. This is called neuroplasticity. Just like physical exercise keeps your muscles strong, cognitive exercise keeps your brain resilient. You can actually train it.

Neuroplasticity — your brain can change

Neuroplasticity is your brain’s lifelong ability to reorganize itself by forming new connections between nerve cells. This isn’t just a theory — it’s measurable. Cognitive training studies in Parkinson’s have used MRI scans to show real structural changes in the brain’s white matter (the brain’s wiring) after just 8 weeks of training (Vriend et al., 2021, Neurotherapeutics).

A key player in this process is BDNF — brain-derived neurotrophic factor. Think of it as your brain’s own fertilizer. Both exercise and mental challenge raise BDNF levels. BDNF helps nerve cells survive and grow, strengthens the connections between them, and has been shown to support the birth of brand-new brain cells in the hippocampus (a key area for memory).

The most exciting finding from dual-task research (doing a physical task and a mental task at the same time) is called the “guided plasticity facilitation hypothesis.” In plain words: moving your body opens the door for your brain to change, and challenging your mind guides where that change happens. Together, they are more powerful than either one alone.

The evidence

What the research actually shows

This is no longer fringe science. Several high-quality randomized controlled trials and large systematic reviews now support cognitive training as a meaningful treatment for people with Parkinson’s.

8-week online cognitive training

Parkinsonism & Related Disorders · 2022 · Amsterdam UMC

van Balkom and colleagues at Amsterdam’s Vrije Universiteit ran a rigorous double-blind randomized controlled trial of 8 weeks of online cognitive training in people with Parkinson’s. It showed measurable improvements in thinking skills and changes in white matter on MRI — meaning the brain physically changed. A 2023 follow-up found that booster sessions are needed to keep the effects long-term, and that people who completed the training had significantly fewer symptoms of depression at follow-up.

Key finding: brain structure measurably changed after 8 weeks.

Computer-based cognitive training — 14 trials

Ageing Research Reviews · 2022 · Systematic review & meta-analysis

Gavelin and colleagues reviewed 14 high-quality randomized controlled trials of computer-based cognitive training in Parkinson’s (following PRISMA 2020 guidelines). They found consistent benefits across several thinking skills, with the strongest evidence for attention, processing speed and executive function. Improvements lasted up to 18 months in studies that included booster sessions.

Key finding: benefits maintained up to 18 months with booster sessions.

Motor–cognitive dual-task training

Brain Sciences · 2023 · Meta-analysis · West China Hospital, Sichuan University

This meta-analysis pooled several randomized trials of dual-task training — doing a physical and a mental task at the same time, like walking while counting backwards. Dual-task training improved both physical and cognitive results in Parkinson’s, and did better than either type of training alone. The proposed reason: exercise raises BDNF and opens the brain to change, while the mental task guides where the new connections form.

Key finding: dual-task training beat physical or cognitive training alone.

Dual-task training — walking, attention & thinking

Archives of Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation · 2023

Wong, Cheng, Yang and colleagues found moderate-quality evidence that dual-task training significantly improved walking speed while dual-tasking, step and stride length, and attention in people with Parkinson’s — compared with usual care or regular physical training alone. This matters because dual-task ability (like walking while talking) is a major factor in fall risk and independence.

Key finding: dual-task walking and attention both improved.

Which cognitive training works — 23 trials

Brain Sciences (MDPI) · 2025 · Systematic review

The most recent 2025 systematic review covered 23 randomized controlled trials from PubMed, PsycINFO and Web of Science. It found strong evidence for many kinds of cognitive training: exergames (video games you play by moving), virtual reality, executive function training, multi-area programs and dual-task training. The authors note that cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s “might lead to an increased risk of developing dementia,” which makes early, steady cognitive training a priority.

Key finding: many approaches work — start early and be consistent.

Physical activity, neuroplasticity & cognitive training

Frontiers in Neuroscience · 2025 · Comprehensive review

This broad 2025 review confirmed that combining physical activity with cognitive training adds up to brain benefits well beyond either one alone. For Parkinson’s specifically, virtual-reality exercise systems with thinking challenges built in improved motor function by 35% compared with regular physiotherapy. The review also points to BDNF — raised by both physical and mental exercise — as a central driver of these brain-changing effects.

Key finding: physical + cognitive training combined is the most powerful approach.

The power of dual-tasking

One of the most exciting insights from recent research is the power of doing two things at once — a thinking task and a physical task at the same time. This goes straight at a known challenge in Parkinson’s: the brain has less capacity to divide attention between competing tasks. That’s why walking and talking can feel so much harder than it used to.

Christine’s tip

Try it yourself today: count backwards from 100 by 3s while walking. Or recite the alphabet while doing gentle exercises. These aren’t party tricks — they’re evidence-based exercises that train exactly the brain–body pathways that Parkinson’s challenges.

The benefits

What cognitive training can do for you

Processing speed

Training improves how fast your brain handles information — one of the first things to slow in Parkinson’s.

Attention & focus

Sustained and divided attention can be meaningfully improved, easing the “brain fog” many people feel.

Executive function

Planning, organizing and problem-solving are all trainable skills that help you stay independent in daily life.

Working memory

Holding and using information in the moment — key for conversations, tasks and finding your way.

Mood & depression

The 2023 van Balkom follow-up found significantly fewer symptoms of depression in the cognitive training group.

Safer walking

Dual-task training directly improves the brain–body coordination you need to walk safely in real-world places.

Brain games

Now let’s put your brain to work!

These games aren’t just for fun — each one targets a thinking skill that Parkinson’s affects. Play them daily for best results. All you need is a pen and paper, and a care partner or friend for some of them.

Targets: working memory · recall

  1. Choose one set of five words below (or ask your partner to read one to you).
  2. Look at the words for 8 seconds, then cover them.
  3. Write down or say every word you remember.
  4. Uncover the list and count how many you got. Try a new set each day and keep track of your best score.

Targets: attention · processing speed · inhibition (stopping an automatic response)

Name the ink colour — not the word! This is the classic Stroop test used in clinical thinking assessments.

  1. Ask your partner to write the colour words RED, BLUE, GREEN and ORANGE on cards, using a marker of a different colour for most of them (for example, the word RED written in blue).
  2. Your partner shows you one card at a time.
  3. Say the colour of the ink as quickly as you can, ignoring what the word says.
  4. Go through 12 cards and time yourself. Try to beat your score next time.

10 or more correct? Outstanding! 7 or more? Well done! Fewer? Keep practising — this one gets easier.

Targets: working memory · processing speed · dual-task preparation

A classic clinical exercise! Count down by 7, starting at 100. This task is used in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) test.

  1. Start at 100 and say each answer out loud: subtract 7 each time.
  2. Go for 10 answers.
  3. Check yourself against the answer key below.

Answer key: 100, 93, 86, 79, 72, 65, 58, 51, 44, 37, 30

9 or 10 correct? Outstanding! 6 or more? Great job! Fewer? This is genuinely hard — keep at it. Once it feels easier, try it while walking or marching on the spot.

Targets: visual memory · attention · sequence processing

Watch a sequence, then repeat it in the same order. The level goes up as you succeed.

  1. Draw a 3 × 3 grid of squares on paper (9 squares).
  2. Your partner taps 2 squares in a row while you watch.
  3. Tap the same squares in the same order.
  4. Each time you get it right, your partner adds one more tap. How many levels can you reach?

Key takeaways

  • Up to 75% of people with Parkinson’s develop thinking changes — but your brain can adapt and change (neuroplasticity).
  • Exercise and mental challenge both raise BDNF, your brain’s own fertilizer.
  • Cognitive training has real research behind it, with benefits lasting up to 18 months when you keep practising.
  • Dual-tasking — moving and thinking at the same time — is more powerful than either one alone, and helps you walk more safely.
  • Start early, be consistent, and play a brain game every day.

The foundation

Nutrition will not cure OA — but it can change everything

Osteoarthritis is not simply “wear and tear” on your joints. Low-grade inflammation, muscle loss and metabolic health all affect how your joints feel and work from day to day. The right nutrition can’t rebuild cartilage (the smooth cushion at the end of your bones). But it can reduce pain signals, keep the muscle that stabilizes your joints, slow decline, and greatly improve how you recover from exercise and daily movement.

The Boxing 4 Health approach

At Boxing 4 Health, we treat nutrition as part of the training, not separate from it. What you eat directly affects how hard you can train, how well you recover, and how protected your joints are during every session. Strong muscles protect joints. Nutrition supports strength. Protein = joint protection

The most important nutrient for OA — especially after 40

Protein does not rebuild cartilage — that’s a myth. What protein does is keep and build muscle, which absorbs shock, stabilizes your joints and protects them from everyday loading. As we age, our bodies get less efficient at using protein. That means adults 40+ need more protein, not less — even if they’re less active than before.

30 g
protein per meal — the target

Research suggests 25–40 g of protein per meal is needed to switch on muscle building effectively in adults 40+. Spreading protein evenly across your meals (not just at dinner) is key for keeping your muscle with OA.

Best evidence

Mediterranean-style eating — the strongest pattern for OA

Of all the eating patterns studied for osteoarthritis, the Mediterranean diet has the most consistent evidence. It supports lower inflammation throughout the body, better metabolic health and better joint function — and, importantly, it’s something you can enjoy and stick with long-term.

Extra-virgin olive oil

Rich in oleocanthal, which works in a similar anti-inflammatory way to ibuprofen at the amounts used in cooking. Use it generously as your main fat.

Fatty fish (2–3 times a week)

Salmon, sardines, mackerel and herring are among the richest food sources of omega-3 fatty acids, which actively reduce joint inflammation.

Vegetables — generously

Fill half your plate with a variety of vegetables at most meals. They give you antioxidants, fibre and anti-inflammatory plant nutrients. Colour variety matters.

Berries and fruit

High in polyphenols and anthocyanins — natural compounds that reduce oxidative stress (cell wear) in joint tissue. Berries, cherries and citrus are especially helpful.

Nuts and seeds

Walnuts, almonds, flaxseed and chia seeds provide healthy fats, fibre and plant-based omega-3s. A small handful a day is a practical, evidence-backed habit.

Whole grains

Swap refined carbohydrates for whole grains where you can. Oats, quinoa, brown rice and whole wheat bread support steady blood sugar and lower inflammation markers.

What to limit

Ultra-processed foods matter more than any “trigger food” list

There is no one arthritis trigger food that applies to everyone. Food sensitivities vary a lot from person to person. But one pattern is clear in the research: highly processed foods, refined sugars and frequent ultra-processed snacks are linked to worse OA symptoms. That’s not because of any single ingredient — it’s because of what they push off your plate, the inflammation they add, and their effect on metabolic health.

Refined sugars and sweetened drinks

Eating a lot of sugar raises inflammation markers and adds to metabolic problems that make OA worse. Sugary drinks are the single most important thing to cut back.

Ultra-processed snacks

Chips, packaged snack foods, fast food and highly processed convenience foods are consistently linked to worse OA. Focus on what you ADD, not just what you remove.

Refined and hydrogenated oils

Industrial seed oils (in fried and ultra-processed foods) are high in omega-6 fatty acids. These promote inflammation when they dominate your diet without omega-3s to balance them.

Alcohol — in moderation

Heavy drinking raises inflammation markers, disturbs sleep, interferes with medication and increases fall risk. For most people, the practical goal is moderation, not cutting it out completely.

The focus shift

Instead of building a long list of foods to avoid, focus on crowding out ultra-processed foods by adding whole, nutrient-rich foods you actually enjoy. When your plate fills up with vegetables, protein and good fats, there’s naturally less room for the things that drive inflammation.

Body composition

The goal is strength — not being smaller

One of the most harmful ideas in managing OA is that weight loss alone will fix joint pain. Losing extra body fat does reduce the load on your joints — but how you lose weight matters enormously. Crash dieting, low-protein eating and extreme calorie cutting all lead to muscle loss. That makes OA worse by taking away the shock absorption and stability your joints depend on.

The wrong approach

Cutting calories hard without enough protein. Putting weight loss ahead of keeping muscle. Avoiding strength training out of fear. Result: less muscle, less stable joints, more pain.

The right approach

Eating enough protein at every meal with a modest calorie balance. Making strength training a priority. Measuring success by strength and function, not the number on the scale. Result: stronger joints, more protection, better quality of life.

Evidence review

Supplements — being realistic about what works

The OA supplement industry is enormous. Most products are heavily marketed but have little clinical evidence. Here’s an honest look at what the research actually supports.

May help some people

Omega-3 fish oil

Lowers inflammation markers. It has the most consistent evidence for reducing OA pain in supplement form. 2–3 g of EPA/DHA daily is the dose that has been researched.

Vitamin D (if you’re low)

Low vitamin D is common and is linked to worse OA and muscle weakness. Get your levels tested before taking a supplement. Correcting a deficiency is important.

Magnesium glycinate

Supports muscle function, sleep quality and pain control. Many adults over 40 are slightly low. It’s well tolerated and affordable.

Not proven cures

Collagen supplements

The marketing is ahead of the evidence. Collagen peptides are broken down during digestion like any other protein. High-quality protein foods have more evidence behind them.

Glucosamine / chondroitin

Decades of trials show mixed results. Some people report symptom relief, but controlled trials don’t show any benefit to the joint structure. Not harmful, but not a cure.

Turmeric / curcumin alone

Some anti-inflammatory effect, but the body absorbs very little of it from a supplement alone. Adding turmeric to food (with black pepper) is reasonable, but it’s not a treatment.

Talk to your care team

This lesson is for education only and isn’t medical advice. Check with your doctor, neurologist or pharmacist before starting a supplement or making a big change to your diet or medication.

Work with your health care team to build a nutrition plan that fits your needs, your medications and your health conditions.

Myth busting

Common OA nutrition myths — debunked

Misinformation about osteoarthritis and nutrition is everywhere. These are the myths I hear most often from clients — and the evidence-based truth behind each one.

The myth: nightshades (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant) cause or worsen arthritis inflammation.

The reality: no clinical evidence supports this. Nightshades are anti-inflammatory vegetables rich in antioxidants. A small number of people may have an individual sensitivity — but this is not OA advice for everyone.

The myth: eating protein damages joints or “feeds” inflammation.

The reality: protein is essential for keeping muscle — which is the main protection for joints with OA. Not getting enough protein is far more harmful for OA than getting too much.

The myth: lifting weights is bad for osteoarthritis and will destroy cartilage faster.

The reality: the opposite is true. Strength training is one of the most effective ways to help OA. Stronger muscles absorb shock and reduce the load on cartilage. Being inactive makes OA worse faster than loading does.

The myth: using only light weights forever is safer for arthritic joints.

The reality: progressive resistance — slowly increasing the load over time — is the goal of any effective exercise program. Joint-safe, progressive loading builds the muscle you need to protect your joints long-term.

The myth: pain during or after exercise always means you are damaging your joints.

The reality: some discomfort during the right kind of exercise is normal and doesn’t mean damage. Telling muscle soreness apart from joint damage — with help from a trained professional — is key to progressing safely.

Key takeaways

  • OA is not just mechanical wear and tear. Inflammation, metabolic health and muscle mass all shape your joint health and pain levels.
  • Protein is your most important nutrient for OA. It keeps your muscle — which stabilizes and protects your joints. Aim for 25–40 g at each meal, spread evenly through the day.
  • The Mediterranean diet has the best evidence for managing OA. Extra-virgin olive oil, fatty fish, vegetables, nuts and berries form the core.
  • Ultra-processed foods and refined sugars consistently worsen OA symptoms. You don’t need a trigger-food list — just less of what comes in a bag or box.
  • The goal is strength and keeping your muscle — not weight loss. Crash dieting and low-protein eating make OA worse by removing your joints’ main protection.
  • Omega-3s and vitamin D (if you’re low) have the best supplement evidence. Collagen, glucosamine and turmeric alone are not cures.
  • Strong muscles protect joints. Nutrition supports strength. Osteoarthritis does not mean fragile.

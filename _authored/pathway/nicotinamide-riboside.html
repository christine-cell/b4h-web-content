Introduction

What is NR — and why does it matter for Parkinson’s?

Nicotinamide riboside (NR) is a naturally occurring form of vitamin B3. Your body uses it as a building block to make NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide). NAD+ is one of the most important molecules in every cell of your body. It powers energy production, repairs damaged DNA, and keeps your mitochondria — the tiny power plants inside your cells — working at their best.

Here’s the challenge: as we age, our NAD+ levels drop significantly. This drop has been directly linked to age-related diseases, including Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s. The exciting possibility with NR is that it offers a practical, safe way to top up NAD+ levels with a supplement — and that’s exactly what researchers have been testing.

The core idea

Mitochondrial problems (struggling cell power plants), neuroinflammation (inflammation in the brain) and poor cell energy are all involved in Parkinson’s. NAD+ helps address all three. NR is a well-tolerated, evidence-backed way to raise your body’s NAD+ levels — and early clinical results in people with Parkinson’s are genuinely promising.

How it works

What NAD+ does in your body

Understanding why NR matters starts with understanding what NAD+ does — and why its decline is such a problem as we age.

  1. Powers cell energy

    NAD+ is essential for your mitochondria to turn food into usable energy. Low levels mean less energy — and with Parkinson’s, mitochondria that are already stressed struggle even more.

  2. Repairs DNA

    NAD+ is a key helper for the enzymes that repair damaged DNA. Nerve cells are especially vulnerable to DNA damage, which makes this job critically important in diseases where nerve cells are lost.

  3. Reduces inflammation

    NAD+ supports the body’s anti-inflammatory pathways. In the NADPARK trial, NR supplements measurably reduced inflammatory cytokines (inflammation signals) in both the blood and the cerebrospinal fluid (the fluid around the brain and spinal cord).

  4. Supports cell clean-up

    NAD+ is involved in lysosomal and proteasomal function — the systems your cells use to clear out damaged proteins, including the alpha-synuclein involved in Parkinson’s.

What the research says — NR in Parkinson’s

The NADPARK study

Cell Metabolism · 2022 · Randomized phase I trial

This landmark double-blind phase I trial, led by Norwegian researchers at Haukeland University Hospital, gave 30 newly diagnosed people with Parkinson’s who had not yet started treatment either 1,000 mg of NR or a placebo every day for 30 days. NR was well tolerated and significantly increased NAD+ levels in the brain. People whose brain NAD+ went up showed measurable changes in brain metabolism on PET scans, along with mild clinical improvement in motor scores (MDS-UPDRS, a standard Parkinson’s rating scale). NR also increased the activity of genes related to mitochondrial, lysosomal and proteasomal function, and reduced inflammatory cytokines in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid.

Key point: selected as one of the Top 10 most influential studies in Cell Metabolism in 2022.

NR-SAFE: high-dose NR is safe

Nature Communications · 2023 · Phase I safety trial

A follow-up phase I trial tested higher doses (3,000 mg/day) in 20 people with Parkinson’s. High-dose NR was found to be safe and produced a strong boost in NAD-related molecules — including up to a 5-fold increase in blood NAD+ levels. People taking NR also showed clinical improvement in total MDS-UPDRS scores. The authors concluded that phase II trials going up to 3,000 mg/day are supported. A larger trial, the NOPARK study, is now recruiting 400 participants for a one-year investigation.

Key point: 5× increase in blood NAD+ levels — safe at 3,000 mg/day.

“NR augmented the NAD metabolome and induced transcriptional upregulation of processes related to mitochondrial, lysosomal, and proteasomal function in blood cells and/or skeletal muscle. Furthermore, NR decreased the levels of inflammatory cytokines in serum and cerebrospinal fluid. Our findings nominate NR as a potential neuroprotective therapy for PD, warranting further investigation in larger trials.”

— Brakedal et al., Cell Metabolism, 2022

Christine’s plain-language translation

It definitely made a meaningful difference in energy function in skeletal muscle and blood — and it reduced brain inflammation too.

Dosage & practical guidance

How to take NR

NR has an excellent safety profile and is well tolerated at the doses tested in clinical trials, up to 3,000 mg per day. That said, I always recommend starting low and building up — and working with your health care provider, especially given how complex Parkinson’s medications can be.

1,000 mg
per day — the dose used in the NADPARK trial
3,000 mg
per day — the highest dose supported by research
Morning
take it in the morning for energy support

What to look for

Choose a reputable brand with third-party testing. NR is sold under brand names including Tru Niagen® (ChromaDex) and Basis® (Elysium). Look for products that list nicotinamide riboside chloride (NR-Cl), the form used in clinical trials.

NR is water-soluble and doesn’t build up in your body — any extra is eliminated naturally. It’s usually taken once a day in the morning, because it supports cell energy and may be mildly stimulating for some people. Always discuss it with your doctor first.

New to my toolkit — and already showing results

This supplement is new to me this year, but it has quickly generated a lot of excitement — and for good reason.

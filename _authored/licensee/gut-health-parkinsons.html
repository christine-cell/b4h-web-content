Constipation in Parkinson's: More Than Just a Nuisance

Up to 80% of people with PD experience constipation, which can worsen quality of life, affect how medications are absorbed, and may even be connected to disease progression.

What Is the Mediterranean Diet?

Section 03

What the Study Found

Eight people with Parkinson's disease followed a Mediterranean diet for 5 weeks, guided by study dietitians. Their live-in partners served as healthy comparison controls. Here's what happened:

Constipation symptoms improved

Participants reported fewer constipation symptoms — including less straining, harder stools, and a reduced sense of incomplete bowel movements — after just 5 weeks on the diet.

The gut microbiome shifted

Stool analysis showed real changes in the types and balance of bacteria living in the gut. Notably, there were increases in bacteria associated with fiber fermentation and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production — chemicals that are helpful for gut health and may even support brain health.

Fiber intake jumped significantly

Participants significantly increased their daily fiber consumption by following the Mediterranean diet. This is particularly important because most people with PD don't consume enough fiber, which directly impacts bowel regularity.

Intestinal permeability was measured

The researchers also checked "leaky gut" markers. While the pilot was too small to draw firm conclusions, the fact that these markers were being tracked reflects how seriously gut integrity is being studied in Parkinson's research.

Good to know

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are produced when gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber. They support the gut lining, help reduce inflammation, and may even have neuroprotective effects — one of the reasons diet is such an active area of Parkinson's research.

Section 04

The Gut–Brain Connection

Scientists increasingly believe that Parkinson's disease may actually begin in the gut — specifically with the misfolding of a protein called alpha-synuclein, which may travel from the gut up to the brain via the vagus nerve. A disrupted gut microbiome (dysbiosis) can trigger inflammation and may contribute to disease progression.

  1. Poor diet

    A diet low in fiber and high in processed foods sets the stage.

  2. Gut dysbiosis

    The balance of gut bacteria is disrupted.

  3. Inflammation

    Dysbiosis can trigger inflammation in the gut.

  4. Increased alpha-synuclein

    Misfolded alpha-synuclein may accumulate and travel via the vagus nerve.

  5. Brain impact

    The process may reach the brain and contribute to disease progression.

Safety

This is a pilot study with a small sample size (n=8). Results are promising but should be considered exploratory. Always work with the person's healthcare team before making significant dietary changes, especially given medication interactions in PD — for example, high-protein meals can affect levodopa absorption.

Key takeaways

  • Constipation is one of the most common non-motor symptoms of Parkinson's and deserves proactive management.
  • A Mediterranean diet — high in fiber, vegetables, and healthy fats — may meaningfully reduce constipation in people with PD.
  • The gut microbiome is modifiable through diet, and changes can happen within weeks.
  • Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by gut bacteria may have neuroprotective effects — another reason diet matters in PD.
  • While this pilot study was small (n=8), a later randomized controlled trial by the same team confirmed key benefits including reduced gut inflammation.
  • Encouraging people to increase fruit, vegetable, legume, and whole grain intake is a low-risk, high-reward strategy.

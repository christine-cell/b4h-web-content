Every great Boxing 4 Health class is built on five non-negotiable principles, captured in the acronym VIGOR. These are not suggestions — they are the must-haves that turn a good workout into a therapeutic experience your clients can't get anywhere else: Voice & Variety, Intensity, Grand Movements, Overall Rotation, and Release.

What you'll learn

  • Why voice work is a therapeutic necessity for people living with Parkinson's — and how to weave it safely into every class.
  • Why intensity of 60–80% of target heart rate is non-negotiable, and how to answer the client who says "I already walk every day."
  • How to coach grand, large-amplitude movements that recalibrate a client's internal sense of "normal."
  • Why trunk rotation matters, and how hands-on correction counters rigidity when words and demos aren't enough.
  • How to program purposeful myofascial release into every cool-down.

The VIGOR Framework

VIGOR is the five-part checklist behind every therapeutic B4H class. Each letter is a must-have — miss one and the class becomes just another workout. Here is the acronym at a glance before we take each letter in turn.

V — Voice & Variety

Use it or lose it. Vocal exercises and constant dialogue keep the voice strong, while variety keeps the brain engaged.

I — Intensity

30–35 minutes at 60–80% of target heart rate. Intensity is what creates therapeutic change.

G — Grand Movements

Large-amplitude, whole-body movement. Everything in class is done BIG.

O — Overall Rotation

Cervical and thoracic rotation to fight the trunk rigidity that cascades through the whole body.

R — Release

Purposeful myofascial release in the cool-down — a therapeutic intervention, not just an ending.

V — Must Have #1

Voice & Variety

With Parkinson's, a student's voice can be affected — but not everyone experiences this. The voice can become quiet, slow, and hard to understand. The more you use it, the less you lose it. Voice work isn't an add-on — it's a therapeutic necessity woven into every class.

It's essential to add vocal exercises during warm-ups and circuits. Create dialogue with your students throughout the session — word games, call-and-response, group exercises, name games. Make it fun, make it social, make it loud.

Safety

Do NOT start with yelling. The voicebox is a muscle too — it needs to be warmed up gradually just like the rest of the body. Start gentle and build intensity through the class.

Voice exercise examples

  1. Group Hum

    Have the class hum a short song together during warm-up — Happy Birthday is perfect. See who can hold their hum the longest. This warms up the vocal cords gently and creates instant group energy.

  2. Word Games

    During warm-up or circuits, play a naming game around the room. Example: "Name car brands using the alphabet — A is for Audi, B is for BMW, C is for Chevrolet…" Go around the room, one student per letter. You can also do vegetables, fruits, cities — anything that gets the brain and voice working together.

  3. Vowel Sounds

    Practice A, E, I, O, U — making sure the whole face is involved and holding each sound as long as possible. Always demo it yourself first so students feel comfortable making silly sounds and noises. Your willingness to look ridiculous gives them permission to do the same.

Good to know

Variety matters too. Neuroplasticity thrives on novelty — doing new things in new ways forces the brain to create new connections. Rotate your voice exercises, word games, and vocal cues so clients are always being pleasantly challenged, never bored.

I — Must Have #2

Intensity

High intensity isn't just about aerobic fitness — it's about the amount of power and output put into every movement. Think about getting off a couch: most of us use adaptations — pushing with our hands, using the armrest, or having someone help. What we're actually lacking is the power and intensity to build momentum. That's exactly what we're training.

Intense exercise — specifically non-contact boxing — has been proven to reverse, reduce, and even delay the symptoms of Parkinson's. We aim to get every client working at 60–80% of their target heart rate during the high-intensity portion of class. Everyone at every level can benefit. Intensity is not negotiable.

Good to know

The 60–80% THR zone is where neuroplastic and cardiovascular benefits are maximized. Below 60% is not sufficient for therapeutic benefit. Above 80% risks fatigue and safety issues for clients with Parkinson's.

Coach's Corner

When a client says, "I stay in shape — I walk every day!", here's your answer: walking is something we need to do every day just to stay alive — it is definitely not enough to help slow the progression of Parkinson's or many other aging-related effects. Walking maintains baseline function. Intensity creates change.

Intensity is not negotiable. Anyone at any level — any stage of Parkinson's, any age, any fitness level — can lessen their symptoms and lead a healthier, happier life when they exercise with intensity. Your job is to help them find their version of it.

G — Must Have #3

Grand Movements

Large amplitude, whole-body movements. Everything in class should be done BIG. Big punches, big steps, big arm swings, big facial expressions, big voice. When we use grand gestures and really emphasize size in every movement, we help the brain recalibrate what "normal" feels like.

People with Parkinson's tend to move smaller than they think they are — their internal sense of movement size is off. When a client tries to move "big" and reaches what feels like 100% effort to them, they might only be at 60% of normal amplitude. Emphasizing grand movements consistently over time helps recalibrate this internal reference point toward normal.

Punches

Full extension on every punch. Arm fully extended, shoulder rotating, weight transferring. No small, tight punches.

Steps

Big, deliberate strides. Heel-toe. High knee lifts when appropriate. Exaggerate the stride — that shuffle becomes a march.

Arm Swings

Counter-rotating arm swing with walking and footwork. Reduced arm swing is a hallmark PD symptom — train it deliberately.

Facial Expressions

PD causes facial masking — reduced expression. Exaggerate vowel sounds, smiles, and surprised faces. The whole face is part of grand movements.

Coach's Corner

Always demo big. If you do small movements, they do small movements. Your energy and amplitude set the standard for the whole room. When you go big, they go big. Lead with your whole body, every single rep.

O — Must Have #4

Overall Rotation

Rigidity is one of the most universal symptoms of Parkinson's — and it's extremely common in seniors too. Clients can experience whole-body stiffness, but the most prominent area is the trunk region. This area has the greatest impact on their ability to rotate — and rotation affects everything.

When trunk rotation is lost…

The shoulders, hips, knees, and back are all forced to overcompensate for what the trunk can't do. This compensation cascade dramatically increases injury risk throughout the entire body — not just the back.

What you must include

At least one exercise targeting rotation in every functional exercise circuit. And during boxing stations, always cue hip rotation — the power of every punch starts in the hips, not the arms.

Most students lack self-awareness about how little they're actually rotating. When you show them the correct range, they think they're already doing it correctly. They genuinely cannot feel the difference.

Coach's Corner

Hands-on correction is often the only thing that works. Ask the student if you can place your hands on their hips and gently move them through the correct range of motion. Let them feel the difference between what they're doing and what rotation actually is. This often needs to happen multiple times across multiple sessions before it starts to stick — and that's completely normal.

R — Must Have #5

Release

Releasing tight fascia improves movement, relieves tightness, and reduces overall tension throughout the body. The cool-down is not just a way to end class — it's a targeted therapeutic intervention in its own right.

Good to know

What is fascia? Fascia is the bands of connective tissue that stabilize, enclose, and separate muscles and organs throughout the entire body. When fascia becomes tight or restricted, it reduces mobility, contributes to pain, and limits the range of motion — compounding the rigidity that PD and aging already create.

We use our cool-downs to pick a specific part of the body and work on it with self-massage balls — loosening areas that most affect alignment and posture. Rotate through these target areas across different sessions:

Back

Upper & lower back muscles.

Chest

Pec muscles & front of shoulder.

Feet

Plantar fascia & arch.

Shoulders

Rotator cuff & rear deltoid.

Glutes

Hip flexors & piriformis.

Neck

Cervical area & traps.

Safety

Don't rush the cool-down. The Release section is 10–15 minutes for a reason. For clients with low blood pressure — common in PD — a rushed transition from intense exercise to standing still can cause dizziness and falls. The cool-down also gives fascia time to respond to the massage work. Never cut it short.

Key takeaways

  • V — Voice & Variety: The voice is a muscle affected by PD; warm it up gradually and use vocal exercises, dialogue, and variety in every class to keep both voice and brain engaged.
  • I — Intensity: 30–35 minutes at 60–80% of target heart rate is where therapeutic change happens. Walking maintains baseline; intensity creates change, and it is not negotiable.
  • G — Grand Movements: Do everything BIG. Consistent large-amplitude movement recalibrates a client's off-kilter sense of "normal" size — and you must demo big to set the standard.
  • O — Overall Rotation: Include at least one rotation exercise per circuit and cue hip rotation in boxing; hands-on correction is often the only way clients truly feel the difference.
  • R — Release: Purposeful 10–15 minute myofascial release with self-massage balls loosens tight fascia and safely eases clients down from intensity. Never cut it short.
